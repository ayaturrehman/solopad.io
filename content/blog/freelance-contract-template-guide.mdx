Looking for a **freelance contract template** you can actually use? This guide walks through every clause your contract needs, with plain-English explanations and sample wording for UK freelancers.

A written contract is not about distrust. It is about clarity. It sets expectations, protects both sides, and answers the question neither party wants to ask: "What happens if this goes wrong?"

**Disclaimer:** This guide is for general education. It is not legal, tax, or accounting advice. Contract law varies by jurisdiction and project type. When in doubt, consult a solicitor.

![Three pillars of a freelance contract: scope, payment, and protection](/blog/freelance-contract-template-guide.svg)

## Why a written freelance contract matters

Verbal agreements feel simpler. Shaking hands over Zoom feels friendly. But when a client cancels mid-project, requests a tenth round of revisions, or goes quiet after you deliver, a clear written contract is the only thing that protects you.

A contract does three things:

1. **Sets boundaries.** It defines what is included and what is not. Scope creep dies when the contract is specific.
2. **Clarifies payment.** Who pays what, when, and how. No surprises.
3. **Protects both sides.** The client knows what they will receive. You know what you are owed.

Most freelance disputes come from ambiguity, not bad intentions. A clear contract removes the ambiguity.

## Clause-by-clause checklist with sample wording

You do not need a 20-page document. You need a clear, simple agreement that covers the important points. Here is what that looks like.

### 1. Parties

Name who is entering the agreement. Use legal names or trading names that match bank accounts and business records.

**Sample wording:**

> This agreement is entered into on [date] between:
> 
> **Freelancer:** [Your name or trading name], [Your address], [Your email]
> 
> **Client:** [Client company or individual name], [Client address], [Client contact email]

**Why it matters:** Clear identification prevents confusion and helps if you ever need to enforce the contract.

### 2. Scope of work

This is the most important clause. Write exactly what you are delivering. Be specific, not vague.

**Bad example:** "Build a website."

**Good example:** "Design and develop a 5-page responsive website (Home, About, Services, Portfolio, Contact) using Next.js and React, with mobile-responsive design, a contact form that sends email to the client's inbox, and basic SEO metadata. Hosted on Vercel."

**Sample wording:**

> **Scope of Work**
> 
> The freelancer will deliver the following:
> 
> ✓ [Specific deliverable 1 — format, platform, specifications]
> ✓ [Specific deliverable 2]
> ✓ [Specific deliverable 3]
> 
> The following are explicitly excluded from this scope: [list anything the client might assume is included but is not, such as ongoing maintenance, content creation, or hosting fees].

**Why it matters:** A clear scope prevents scope creep. The more specific you are now, the fewer arguments you will have later.

### 3. Fee, currency, and VAT status

How much. In what currency. Whether VAT applies.

**Sample wording:**

> **Fee**
> 
> The total project fee is **£4,800 GBP**, exclusive of VAT.
> 
> [OR, if you are not VAT registered: The total project fee is **£4,800 GBP**. The freelancer is not VAT registered.]

**Why it matters:** Stating the currency and VAT status prevents confusion, especially for clients who work across borders or expect VAT invoices.

### 4. Deposit and payment schedule

When and how the client pays. Always take a deposit before starting work.

**Sample wording:**

> **Payment Schedule**
> 
> ✓ **Deposit:** 50% (£2,400) due before work begins.
> ✓ **Milestone 1:** 25% (£1,200) due upon delivery of homepage and navigation (target date: 15 September 2026).
> ✓ **Final payment:** 25% (£1,200) due upon delivery of all pages, testing, and launch (target date: 30 September 2026).
> 
> Payment method: Bank transfer to [your bank details] or payment link provided in invoice.

**Why it matters:** A deposit signals the client is serious. A milestone structure keeps cash flowing and gives the client visibility into progress.

**UK note:** For commercial clients, you can reference Net 7 or Net 14 terms (payment due within 7 or 14 days of invoice). Make the due date explicit on every invoice.

### 5. Timeline and milestones

When the project starts, when it ends, and what the checkpoints are along the way.

**Sample wording:**

> **Timeline**
> 
> Project start: 1 September 2026  
> Milestone 1 delivery: 15 September 2026  
> Final delivery: 30 September 2026
> 
> Delays caused by late client deliverables (content, brand assets, feedback, logins) will extend the timeline by an equivalent number of days.

**Why it matters:** Dates keep the project moving. The clause about client delays protects you when they take three weeks to send their logo.

### 6. Revisions

How many rounds of changes are included, and what happens after that.

**Sample wording:**

> **Revisions**
> 
> Two rounds of revisions are included in the project fee. Additional revisions will be billed at £85 per hour.
> 
> A "round" is defined as one consolidated set of feedback from the client. Multiple emails or calls within the same revision phase count as one round.

**Why it matters:** Without this clause, you risk endless revision requests. Two rounds is standard. Three is generous. Unlimited is a trap.

### 7. Change requests and scope creep

What happens when the client asks for something outside the original scope.

**Sample wording:**

> **Change Requests**
> 
> Requests for work outside the agreed scope will be quoted separately and added to the project as a written change order. Work on changes will begin only after the client approves the additional cost and timeline impact.

**Why it matters:** Scope creep is the number one reason freelance projects go sideways. This clause stops it before it starts.

### 8. Kill fee / cancellation

What you are owed if the client cancels mid-project.

**Sample wording:**

> **Cancellation and Kill Fee**
> 
> If the client cancels the project after work has begun, the client agrees to pay for all completed work at the project's hourly equivalent rate (£80/hour), with a minimum of 30% of the total project fee (£1,440).
> 
> The deposit is non-refundable once work has started.

**Why it matters:** This protects you from doing weeks of work and getting nothing. It also makes clients think twice before pulling the plug on a whim.

### 9. Intellectual property transfer

Who owns the work, and when.

**Sample wording:**

> **Intellectual Property**
> 
> All intellectual property rights in the final deliverables transfer to the client upon receipt of final payment in full. Until full payment is received, all work remains the property of the freelancer.
> 
> The freelancer retains ownership of any pre-existing materials, templates, or code libraries used in the project.

**Why it matters:** The client owns the final work after they pay. Not before. This is your leverage. Do not give it away before you are paid.

### 10. Confidentiality

Both sides keep sensitive information private.

**Sample wording:**

> **Confidentiality**
> 
> Both parties agree to keep confidential any proprietary or sensitive information shared during the project, including but not limited to business plans, pricing, and client data.

**Why it matters:** Simple protection for both sides. Two sentences is enough.

### 11. Late payment

What happens if the client pays late.

**Sample wording:**

> **Late Payment**
> 
> Invoices unpaid after 14 days of the due date will incur a late fee of 3% per month (36% per annum) on the outstanding balance.
> 
> Under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998, freelancers trading with UK businesses have statutory rights to interest and recovery costs. The terms above reflect our standard practice.

**Why it matters:** The Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 gives UK freelancers statutory rights when commercial clients pay late. Your contract terms make expectations clear and add a specific rate. The point is not to make money from late fees; it is to motivate on-time payment.

**UK context (high level):** For commercial contracts (B2B), the Act provides a statutory interest rate (currently Bank of England base rate + 8%) and allows recovery of certain costs. For consumer contracts (B2C), different rules apply. This guide is not legal advice — when in doubt, consult a solicitor.

### 12. Communication windows

How and when you will communicate.

**Sample wording:**

> **Communication**
> 
> Communication will happen via email and scheduled video calls during business hours (Monday–Friday, 9am–6pm GMT). Response time: within 24 hours on business days.

**Why it matters:** Sets boundaries. Prevents 11pm Saturday phone calls.

### 13. Client responsibilities and deliverables

The project does not just depend on you. The client needs to provide content, feedback, logins, and assets.

**Sample wording:**

> **Client Responsibilities**
> 
> The client will provide:
> 
> ✓ All page content and brand assets by 5 September 2026
> ✓ Access credentials (hosting, domain, analytics) by 8 September 2026
> ✓ Feedback on each milestone within 5 business days of delivery
> 
> Delays in client deliverables will extend the project timeline by an equivalent number of days.

**Why it matters:** If the client takes three weeks to send their logo, that is not your fault. Without this clause, you will be the one apologising for the delay.

### 14. Portfolio rights

Can you show this work in your portfolio?

**Sample wording:**

> **Portfolio Rights**
> 
> The freelancer retains the right to display the completed work in their portfolio, case studies, and marketing materials, with appropriate credit given to the client unless otherwise requested.

**Why it matters:** Most clients do not care. But the one who does will care a lot. Get it agreed upfront.

### 15. Independent contractor / employment status (UK)

A short note clarifying you are not an employee.

**Sample wording:**

> **Independent Contractor**
> 
> The freelancer is an independent contractor and not an employee of the client. The freelancer is responsible for their own tax, National Insurance, and VAT obligations (if applicable).
> 
> This agreement does not create an employment relationship. IR35 and off-payroll working rules may apply to certain contracts — both parties are responsible for their own compliance.

**Why it matters:** This is an honest, high-level statement. It does not solve IR35 or guarantee your status, but it clarifies the intent of the relationship. For IR35-sensitive contracts (especially public sector or large corporates), consult an accountant or specialist adviser.

### 16. Governing law and jurisdiction

Which country's law applies.

**Sample wording:**

> **Governing Law**
> 
> This agreement is governed by the law of England and Wales [OR Scotland, OR Northern Ireland], and both parties submit to the exclusive jurisdiction of the courts of [England and Wales / Scotland / Northern Ireland].

**Why it matters:** Makes enforcement clearer if a dispute arises.

## Worked example: £4,800 website project

Here is how the numbers work in a real project.

**Project:** 5-page website for a consultancy (design, development, testing, launch)

**Total fee:** £4,800 GBP (exclusive of VAT; freelancer not VAT registered)

**Payment schedule:**

- **Deposit (50%):** £2,400 due before kickoff
- **Milestone 1 (25%):** £1,200 due on delivery of homepage and navigation
- **Final payment (25%):** £1,200 due on delivery of all pages, testing, and launch

**Kill fee:** If the client cancels after work starts, they pay for completed work at £80/hour (the project's implied hourly rate), with a minimum of 30% (£1,440). The deposit (£2,400) is non-refundable.

**Revisions:** Two rounds included. Additional revisions billed at £85/hour.

**Late payment:** 3% per month on unpaid invoices after 14 days.

**IP transfer:** Client owns all final deliverables after full payment. Freelancer retains rights to any pre-existing templates or code libraries.

**Timeline:** 4 weeks from kickoff to launch, with milestones at weeks 2 and 4. Client delays extend the timeline by an equal number of days.

This structure protects the freelancer's cash flow, sets clear expectations, and gives the client visibility into progress and costs.

## How to send a contract without awkwardness

This is the part many freelancers worry about. "Won't the client think I don't trust them?"

No. They will think you are professional. Every serious freelancer uses contracts. If a client pushes back on signing one, that is not a client you want.

**Script you can adapt:**

> "Before we kick off, I'll send over a short agreement that covers the scope, timeline, and payment terms we discussed. It protects us both and makes sure we're on the same page. I'll email it today — just reply with your approval or let me know if you'd like to talk through any of the points."

That is it. No drama. No awkwardness. Just professionalism.

If the client says "Do we really need a contract?", you can say:

> "It's just a clear record of what we've agreed. If everything goes smoothly, we never need to look at it again. But if there's ever a question about scope or payment, we'll both be glad we have it in writing."

Most clients will respect this. The ones who resist are often the ones you will have problems with later.

## Free freelance contract template checklist

Use this as your master outline. Fill in the blanks for your next project.

✓ **Parties** — your legal name and the client's name + address  
✓ **Scope of work** — exact deliverables (pages, formats, platforms)  
✓ **Fee & currency** — total price in GBP (or other currency), VAT status  
✓ **Deposit** — 40–50% upfront, non-refundable after work starts  
✓ **Payment schedule** — milestones and due dates  
✓ **Timeline** — start date, milestones, final delivery date  
✓ **Revisions** — number of rounds included + hourly rate after that  
✓ **Change requests** — how scope changes are handled  
✓ **Kill fee** — what you are owed if they cancel mid-project  
✓ **IP ownership** — transfers only after final payment  
✓ **Confidentiality** — both sides keep pricing and business info private  
✓ **Late fees** — e.g. 3% per month after 14 days (UK Late Payment Act context)  
✓ **Communication** — channels and response windows  
✓ **Client deliverables** — content, assets, feedback deadlines  
✓ **Portfolio rights** — whether you can show the work publicly  
✓ **Independent contractor** — honest note about employment status and IR35  
✓ **Governing law** — England & Wales (or Scotland / Northern Ireland)

A simple one-page version covering these points beats a blank Google Doc every time.

## A contract is not the enemy. Ambiguity is.

Every freelance horror story — scope creep, ghosted payments, endless revisions, stolen work — could have been prevented with a clear contract.

A contract will not guarantee a perfect project. Nothing does. But it gives you a foundation. Something to point to when expectations do not match. Something that says: "This is what we agreed."

That is worth more than any handshake.

Templates are fine. Just customise them. Start with the structure above. Change the scope, the price, the dates. Keep the rest.

What matters is that it exists, it is clear, and it is signed before work begins.
