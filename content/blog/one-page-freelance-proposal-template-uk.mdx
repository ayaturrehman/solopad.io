You send a client a 12-page proposal. They reply three weeks later: "Sorry, I never got round to reading it."

Or you send a two-line quote. They reply immediately: "Can you explain what is included? I got another quote for less."

Both scenarios waste time. The first because your proposal was too long. The second because your quote was too vague.

What UK freelancers need is a **one-page proposal** — long enough to show you understand the problem and what you will deliver, short enough that a busy client can read it in 90 seconds and say yes.

This guide gives you a copy-paste one-page proposal template for UK freelancers, explains when to use a one-page proposal instead of a quote or full contract, shows three worked GBP examples (designer package, developer day-rate, consultant discovery), includes an acceptance block with "reply YES or sign" wording, and provides a 5-line send email script.

## The problem with long proposals (and thin quotes)

Most UK freelancers swing between two extremes:

**Extreme 1: The 8-page proposal deck**

You spend four hours writing a proposal with case studies, process diagrams, testimonials, a detailed timeline, terms and conditions, and a branded cover page. The client opens the PDF, sees 8 pages, and closes it. They meant to come back to it. They never do.

**Extreme 2: The three-line quote**

> "Website redesign: £3,500. Let me know if you want to proceed."

No context. No scope boundaries. No deposit structure. The client replies: "I got a quote for £1,800. Why are you more expensive?" You have no space to justify the price because you never explained what you were delivering or why.

Both extremes fail because they do not match how small business clients make decisions. They do not want to read a dissertation. But they do need more than a number.

They need: **Problem → Solution → Price → Next step.** That is what a one-page proposal delivers.

## What a one-page proposal is (and what it is not)

A **one-page proposal** is a structured document under 400 words that captures:

✓ **The problem** the client is facing (in their words, not yours)  
✓ **The outcome** you will deliver (specific, not "we will help you succeed")  
✓ **Scope bullets** (what is included)  
✓ **Out-of-scope note** (what is NOT included, to prevent scope creep)  
✓ **Timeline** (start date and delivery date)  
✓ **GBP investment** (price, deposit %, VAT if applicable)  
✓ **Acceptance path** (how the client says yes: reply, sign, or deposit payment)  

### What a one-page proposal is NOT

✗ A quote (quotes list prices but do not explain the problem or solution)  
✗ A contract (contracts define legal terms; proposals win the work)  
✗ A scope of work (SOWs come after the proposal is accepted)  
✗ A sales pitch (no "we are a leading agency" or "our award-winning team")  

A one-page proposal sits between the enquiry and the contract. It answers: **"Should I hire this freelancer?"** Not: **"What are the legal terms?"** (that is the contract's job) or **"What does this freelancer charge?"** (that is a quote).

## Decision test — quote vs one-page proposal vs full SOW/contract

Not every project needs a one-page proposal. Here is when to use each format.

### Use a **quote** when:

✓ The client knows exactly what they want and just needs a price (e.g. "How much to design 10 Instagram templates?")  
✓ The project is under £500 and straightforward  
✓ You have worked with the client before and they trust your scope judgement  

**Example quote:**

> 10 Instagram templates (1080×1080px, editable Canva files): £400. Delivery: 5 business days. Reply YES to confirm.

### Use a **one-page proposal** when:

✓ The client has a problem but has not defined the solution yet  
✓ The project is £1,000–£10,000 and moderately complex  
✓ You need to clarify scope boundaries to prevent scope creep  
✓ The client is comparing you to other freelancers and needs to see why you are the right choice  

**Example scenario:** A café owner says "I need a website." You do not know if they mean a 3-page site or a 10-page booking system. A one-page proposal forces you (and them) to define that before you quote.

### Use a **full proposal + SOW/contract** when:

✓ The project is over £10,000  
✓ Multiple stakeholders need to approve (board, committee, procurement team)  
✓ The timeline is longer than 3 months  
✓ Legal terms (IP ownership, liability, termination) are critical and need a separate contract  

For full contract templates and clause guidance, see [freelance contract template guide](/blog/freelance-contract-template-guide). For post-win scope-of-work templates, see [freelance scope of work template](/blog/freelance-scope-of-work-template).

Most UK freelancers overuse full proposals when a one-page version would close the deal faster.

## One-page proposal structure — field by field

Here is the structure that works for most UK freelancers. Adjust the wording to fit your discipline, but keep the order.

### Section 1: Header (client + your details)

**Proposal for:** [Client name or company name]  
**Prepared by:** [Your name]  
**Date:** [Date sent]  
**Valid until:** [7–14 days from send date]  

The "valid until" date creates urgency without pressure. If the client has not decided by that date, follow up once. If they still cannot commit, walk away or reprice.

### Section 2: Problem + outcome

**What you are solving:**  
Restate the client's problem in 1–2 sentences. Prove you listened. Use their words where possible.

Example:  
_"Your current website does not convert enquiries. Visitors land on the homepage and leave without contacting you. You need a site that turns traffic into leads."_

**What you will deliver:**  
State the outcome in business terms, not technical jargon. The client does not care about "responsive CSS" — they care about "a site that works on mobile."

Example:  
_"A redesigned 5-page website optimised for conversions, with a clear call-to-action on every page and a contact form that feeds directly into your CRM."_

### Section 3: Scope (included + excluded)

**What is included:**

Use tick bullets (✓) to list deliverables. Be specific. "Brand identity" is vague. "Logo, colour palette, typography guide, and social media templates" is clear.

Example:

✓ 5-page website (Home, About, Services, Portfolio, Contact)  
✓ Mobile-responsive design  
✓ Contact form with email notifications  
✓ Basic on-page SEO (meta titles, descriptions)  
✓ 1 round of revisions  

**What is NOT included (out of scope):**

This line prevents scope creep. State what you will NOT do. Clients assume features are included unless you say otherwise.

Example:  
_"Not included: e-commerce, blog setup, copywriting, ongoing hosting, or additional revision rounds beyond 1."_

### Section 4: Timeline

**Start date:** [e.g. "Week commencing 7 October 2026"]  
**Delivery date:** [e.g. "22 October 2026"]  

If the timeline depends on the client providing content or feedback, say so:  
_"Timeline assumes you provide content and feedback within 2 business days of each milestone."_

### Section 5: Investment (GBP price + VAT + deposit)

**Total investment:** £[amount]  

If VAT applies:  
**Ex VAT:** £[amount]  
**Plus VAT (20%):** £[VAT amount]  
**Total:** £[total inc VAT]  

If VAT does NOT apply:  
**Total:** £[amount] _(VAT not applicable)_  

**Deposit:** To secure your start date, a deposit of £[amount] ([%] of total) is due upon acceptance. The remaining £[amount] is invoiced upon project completion.

**Payment terms:** [e.g. "Net 7 days" or "Due on receipt"]

For full deposit invoice templates and balance invoice wording, see [deposit invoice template UK](/blog/deposit-invoice-template-uk).

### Section 6: How to accept this proposal

**To proceed:**  
Reply to this email with "YES" or sign below and return this document. Once I receive your confirmation and deposit payment, I will secure your start date and send a contract for your records.

**Signature (optional):**  
If you want a formal acceptance, add:

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_  
[Client name], [Date]  

**Bank details for deposit:**  
Sort code: [XX-XX-XX]  
Account number: [XXXXXXXX]  
Account name: [Your name or business name]  

Or: "Invoice will be sent upon acceptance."

**Valid until:** [Date]

After this date, pricing and availability may change. If you need more time, let me know and I can extend the validity.

## The acceptance block — how to make "yes" frictionless

Most proposals fail because the acceptance path is unclear. The client reads your proposal, likes it, and then... what?

Do they reply? Sign something? Pay a deposit first? Wait for you to send a contract?

If you do not tell them, they will wait for you to tell them. And while they wait, another freelancer with a clearer next step wins the project.

### Three acceptance paths that work

**Option 1: Reply YES**

Simplest option. No forms, no signatures, just a reply.

> "To proceed, reply to this email with 'YES'. Once I receive your confirmation and deposit payment, I will secure your start date and send a contract for your records."

**Option 2: Sign and return**

Works for clients who prefer formal acceptance or need a paper trail for their finance team.

> "To proceed, sign below and return this document (scan or PDF). Once I receive your signed proposal and deposit payment, I will secure your start date."

Add a signature block:

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_  
[Client name], [Date]  

**Option 3: Pay the deposit**

Fastest close. If they pay, they have committed.

> "To proceed, pay the deposit of £[amount] using the bank details below. Once I receive payment, I will send you a contract and secure your start date."

Provide bank details:

Sort code: [XX-XX-XX]  
Account number: [XXXXXXXX]  
Account name: [Your name or business name]  

Or: "Invoice will be sent upon acceptance."

### Do NOT say:

✗ "Let me know if you are interested." (Too passive. You have already sent a proposal — of course they are interested.)  
✗ "Feel free to reach out with questions." (Again, passive. Give them an action, not permission.)  
✗ "I look forward to hearing from you." (Same problem. No clear next step.)

Say: **"Reply YES to proceed."** Clear. Simple. Frictionless.

## The send email — 5 lines that close deals

Your one-page proposal is ready. Now you need a short email to send it with. Here is the structure that works.

---

**Subject:** Proposal for [Project name] — [Client company name]

> Hi [Client name],
>
> Thanks for the call earlier. As discussed, I have attached a one-page proposal for [brief project description, e.g. "your website redesign" or "the brand identity project"].
>
> It includes scope, timeline, pricing, and how to proceed. The proposal is valid until [date], so let me know if you have any questions before then.
>
> If you are ready to move forward, just reply YES and I will send the deposit invoice and contract.
>
> Looking forward to working with you.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

**Attachment:** [Your one-page proposal PDF or link to Google Doc]

---

### What NOT to include in the send email

✗ An apology ("Sorry for the delay")  
✗ A sales pitch (your proposal already did that)  
✗ A link to book another call (you already had the call)  
✗ A request for feedback ("Let me know what you think")  

The email is a delivery vehicle, not a sales letter. Keep it short.

## What NOT to put on a one-page proposal (and where to put it instead)

A one-page proposal is not your portfolio, CV, or terms and conditions document. Here is what to leave out — and where it belongs instead.

### Leave OUT of the proposal:

✗ **Your full portfolio or case studies** → Put these in your initial pitch email or website link  
✗ **Testimonials or client logos** → Share these on your website or LinkedIn, not the proposal  
✗ **Your design process or methodology** → Clients do not care about your 5-step process; they care about the outcome  
✗ **Legal terms (IP ownership, liability, termination clauses)** → Save these for the contract after the proposal is accepted  
✗ **Your CV or full bio** → They already decided to ask you for a proposal; they know who you are  

The proposal answers one question: **"Should I hire this freelancer for this specific project?"** Everything else is noise.

## How to handle pushback on price (without defending yourself)

Client replies: "Your price is higher than I expected. Can you reduce it?"

Bad response:  
_"I understand, but my rate reflects my experience and the value I bring. I have been doing this for 10 years and..."_

That is defensive. You are justifying your existence. Do not do that.

Good response:  
_"I can adjust the scope to fit your budget. Which deliverables are most important to you, and which could we drop or move to a second phase?"_

This shifts the conversation from "you are too expensive" to "what fits your budget?"

If they say: "I need everything but cheaper," walk away. That client will not value your work.

## When a one-page proposal is NOT enough — and what to add

A one-page proposal works for most projects under £10,000. But sometimes you need more. Here is when to expand beyond one page.

### Add a second page when:

✓ The timeline has multiple phases or dependencies  
✓ You need to list assumptions (e.g. "Client provides all content by [date]" or "Hosting costs are client's responsibility")  
✓ The client specifically requested a detailed breakdown of tasks or milestones  

### Send a separate contract when:

✓ The project is over £5,000  
✓ IP ownership or liability matters (e.g. software development, branding)  
✓ The client is a large company or public sector body that requires formal terms  

The proposal wins the work. The contract protects both sides. Do not try to combine them on one page.
