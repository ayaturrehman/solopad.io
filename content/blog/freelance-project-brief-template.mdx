A prospective client emails you: "Hi, I need a website. Can you send a quote?"

You pause. A website could mean anything. Five pages or fifty. Custom build or template customisation. £2,000 or £20,000. E-commerce, portfolio, booking system, blog.

You have two options:

1. **Guess the scope** and send a vague quote that is either too high (and you lose the lead) or too low (and you work unpaid hours later when the client's real requirements emerge).
2. **Send a project brief** — a structured form that captures exactly what the client needs, what their budget is, and when they need it — so your proposal is accurate and your scope is protected from day one.

Most UK freelancers guess. The smart ones send a project brief first.

This guide gives you a copy-paste freelance project brief template, explains when and how to send it, includes email scripts for getting clients to fill it in (and what to do when they refuse), shows worked GBP examples, and includes decision tests for when a thin brief means walk away or raise your deposit.

## What a project brief is (and why it stops scope creep before it starts)

A **project brief** (also called a **client brief** or **intake form**) is a structured document or form you send to a prospective client to gather the information you need before you write a proposal or quote.

It is not a creative brief (that comes later, after the client hires you). It is not a scope of work (that also comes later, after they accept your proposal). It is the **intake step** between "I am interested" and "Here is your quote."

### What a project brief captures

✓ **What** the client needs (deliverables, features, outcomes)  
✓ **Why** they need it (business goals, audience, problem being solved)  
✓ **When** they need it (deadlines, launch dates, urgency)  
✓ **Budget range** in GBP (so you know if the project is viable)  
✓ **Who** makes the final decision (so you know whose approval matters)  
✓ **Technical or logistical constraints** (hosting, brand guidelines, integrations, existing assets)  

### How it prevents scope creep

Scope creep starts at the enquiry stage. A client says "I need a logo" and you quote £800. You win the project. Then they ask for business cards. Then Instagram templates. Then a pitch deck. None of that was in the original enquiry, but because you never captured what was included and what was not, the client assumes it is all part of the £800.

A project brief forces the client to define the boundaries **before you quote**. If they want logo plus business cards plus templates, that goes in the brief. Your proposal reflects the full scope. No surprises. No unpaid extras.

### When to send a project brief

Send a project brief after the initial enquiry but before you write a proposal.

**Example timeline:**

1. **Client enquiry:** "I need a website. What do you charge?"  
2. **Your reply:** "I would love to help. To give you an accurate quote, I need a few details about the project. Could you fill in this brief?"  
3. **Client fills in brief:** Budget £5,000–£8,000, 10 pages, e-commerce, launch by December.  
4. **You review the brief:** The scope is clear. You can do this. Budget works.  
5. **You send a proposal:** £6,500, 6 weeks, milestone payment schedule, scope of work attached.  

Without step 2–4, you would have guessed. With the brief, your proposal is accurate and your cashflow is protected.

## Project brief vs creative brief vs scope of work vs proposal — the differences

These terms overlap, and clients often confuse them. Here is how they differ and when each is used.

| Document | Sent by | Sent when | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Project brief** | Freelancer to client | Before quoting | Capture requirements so you can write an accurate proposal |
| **Proposal** | Freelancer to client | After project brief is reviewed | Pitch your approach, fee, and timeline to win the project |
| **Creative brief** | Client to freelancer (or filled in together) | After contract signed, before work begins | Define creative direction, tone, audience, competitors |
| **Scope of work** | Freelancer to client | After proposal accepted, before work begins | Define deliverables, exclusions, revisions, and boundaries to prevent scope creep |

**In short:**  
✓ **Project brief** = enquiry stage (you ask the client for details)  
✓ **Proposal** = sales stage (you pitch your solution)  
✓ **Scope of work** = post-win stage (you define boundaries before starting work)  
✓ **Creative brief** = creative direction stage (after the contract is signed)

## What to include in your project brief — field-by-field checklist

Your project brief should be thorough but not overwhelming. Aim for 10–15 questions that take the client 10–15 minutes to complete.

Here is the structure most UK freelancers use, broken down by section.

### Section 1: Client and project overview

✓ **Client name and company**  
✓ **Contact email and phone**  
✓ **Project name or working title**  
✓ **Industry or sector** (e.g. hospitality, tech, professional services, retail)  
✓ **How did you hear about me?** (referral, Google, portfolio, LinkedIn — useful for tracking lead sources)  

### Section 2: Project goals and audience

✓ **What problem are you solving or goal are you achieving?**  
✓ **Who is the target audience?** (age, location, job title, buying behaviour)  
✓ **What does success look like for this project?** (more leads, better conversions, rebrand perception, launch new service)  

### Section 3: Deliverables and scope

✓ **What specific deliverables do you need?** (e.g. logo and brand guidelines; 10-page website with e-commerce; product launch campaign)  
✓ **Do you have existing brand assets we should use or reference?** (logo, colours, fonts, photography)  
✓ **Are there any technical requirements or constraints?** (hosting provider, CMS preference, integrations, accessibility standards)  
✓ **Do you have examples or competitor sites you admire?** (links to 2–3 references)  

### Section 4: Timeline and decision-making

✓ **When do you need this project completed?** (specific date or "as soon as possible")  
✓ **Is this deadline fixed or flexible?**  
✓ **Who will approve the work and make final decisions?** (you, your marketing director, board approval required)  
✓ **How quickly can you typically provide feedback?** (same day, 2–3 days, 1 week)  

### Section 5: Budget

✓ **What is your budget range for this project?** (provide GBP ranges as examples: e.g. £2,000–£5,000, £5,000–£10,000, £10,000+, or "not sure yet")  

This is the most important question. Clients often skip it. Do not let them. Budget clarity determines whether you spend time on a proposal or politely decline.

### Section 6: Additional context

✓ **Is there anything else we should know about this project?**  
✓ **Are there any concerns or challenges you are navigating?** (e.g. tight budget, past bad experience with freelancers, internal stakeholder alignment issues)  

This open-ended section often reveals risks or opportunities you would not discover otherwise.

## Real worked example — brand identity brief with GBP budget

Let's walk through a completed project brief for a brand identity project.

**Client:** Northline Fitness (gym and personal training studio, Bristol)  
**Freelancer:** Oak & Pixel (brand designer, fictional example)  
**Project:** Brand identity for new fitness studio opening in December 2026  

### Client and project overview

**Your name:** Sarah Jennings  
**Company name:** Northline Fitness  
**Email:** sarah@northlinefitness.co.uk  
**Phone:** 07700 900123  
**Project name:** Brand identity for Northline Fitness studio launch  
**Industry or sector:** Health and fitness (boutique gym and personal training)  
**How did you hear about me?** Referral from a friend who worked with you last year  

### Project goals and audience

**What problem are you solving or goal are you achieving?**  
We are opening a new fitness studio in Bristol in December and need a professional brand identity to attract members before launch. We want to stand out from budget gyms and position ourselves as a premium, welcoming space for people who are new to fitness or returning after a break.

**Who is the target audience?**  
Adults aged 28–50, Bristol-based, professional or mid-career, looking for personal attention and a non-intimidating gym environment. Not hardcore athletes — more "I want to feel healthy and confident" than "I want to compete."

**What does success look like?**  
A brand identity that feels modern, welcoming, and premium (but not intimidating). We want people to see our logo and website and think: "This is the kind of place I would feel comfortable joining."

### Deliverables and scope

**What specific deliverables do you need?**  
✓ Logo (icon + wordmark)  
✓ Colour palette  
✓ Typography (fonts for signage, website, and print)  
✓ Brand guidelines PDF (10–15 pages covering logo usage, colour, tone of voice, and examples)  
✓ Social media profile images (Instagram, Facebook)  

**Do you have existing brand assets?**  
No, we are starting from scratch. We have a name (Northline Fitness) but no logo or visual identity yet.

**Are there any technical requirements or constraints?**  
The logo needs to work well at small sizes (app icons, social media) and large sizes (gym signage, vehicle graphics). We will also need print-ready files because we are getting T-shirts and posters printed locally.

**Do you have examples of brands or designs you admire?**  
✓ Third Space (London gym) — premium but not intimidating  
✓ Peloton branding — modern, clean, motivating  
✓ Local coffee shop "Grounded" (Bristol) — friendly, approachable, not corporate  

### Timeline and decision-making

**When do you need this project completed?**  
By mid-November 2026 (we launch in December and need the brand ready for website build and signage production).

**Is this deadline fixed or flexible?**  
Fixed — we have already booked the signage company and website developer for late November.

**Who will approve the work?**  
Me (Sarah) and my business partner (Tom). We make decisions together, usually within 2–3 days.

**How quickly can you typically provide feedback?**  
2–3 business days. We meet twice a week to review freelancer work, so feedback will be structured and consistent.

### Budget

**What is your budget range for this project?**  
£3,000–£5,000. We have allocated £4,000 internally, but we have some flexibility if the scope justifies it.

### Additional context

**Is there anything else we should know?**  
We want to avoid clichés (dumbbells, muscular silhouettes, aggressive fonts). We are trying to create a gym that feels inclusive and supportive, not hardcore. We are also working with a web designer separately, so we will need to hand off the brand guidelines and logo files to them in November.

**Are there any concerns or challenges?**  
Timeline is tight, so we need a designer who can meet deadlines and provide feedback windows that work with our schedule. We had a previous designer ghost us after the deposit, so we are cautious about commitment and communication.

---

### What the freelancer does next

The brief is clear. Budget is £3,000–£5,000, timeline is 6 weeks, scope is logo + brand guidelines + social assets. The freelancer reviews it and decides:

✓ Scope is achievable within budget and timeline  
✓ Client has realistic expectations and clear decision-making process  
✓ Red flag noted (previous bad experience) but client is transparent about it  

The freelancer sends a proposal: **£4,200, 6 weeks, 40% deposit upfront, milestone invoicing, scope of work attached.**

Client accepts. Project starts. No scope creep, because the brief defined everything upfront.

## Email scripts — how to send a project brief and handle pushback

### Script 1 — Sending the project brief (initial enquiry response)

**Subject:** Re: [Project name] — Quick brief before I quote

> Hi [Client name],
>
> Thanks for reaching out. I would love to help with [brief description of what they mentioned, e.g. "your new website" or "the rebrand for your consultancy"].
>
> To give you an accurate quote and timeline, I need a few details about the project. I have attached a project brief (10–15 questions, takes about 15 minutes to complete).
>
> Once I receive your completed brief, I will review it and send you a detailed proposal within [2–3 business days / 1 week].
>
> If you have any questions while filling it in, just reply to this email and I will clarify.
>
> Looking forward to working with you.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

**Attach:** Your project brief as a Google Doc (with comment access), PDF (they fill in and return), or link to a Typeform / Notion form.

### Script 2 — When a client says "Just send a rough quote first"

**Subject:** Re: [Project name] — Why I need a brief before quoting

> Hi [Client name],
>
> I completely understand wanting a rough idea of cost before committing time to a brief.
>
> The challenge is that "rough quotes" often become fixed expectations — and if I quote too low without understanding the full scope, I end up undercharging (which is not fair to me), or if I quote too high, you might assume I am too expensive when in reality the project might fit your budget once we define it properly.
>
> The brief takes 10–15 minutes and ensures my proposal is accurate, fair, and tailored to what you actually need. That saves us both time.
>
> If it helps, I can give you a general range based on similar projects: [e.g. "Most brand identity projects I work on are £3,000–£6,000 depending on scope. Website builds start at £5,000."]
>
> Does that help? If you are comfortable with that range, I would love to see your completed brief so I can give you a precise quote.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

### Script 3 — When a client refuses to fill in the brief

**Subject:** Re: [Project name] — Alternative to the brief

> Hi [Client name],
>
> No problem — I understand the brief might feel like extra admin.
>
> How about we schedule a 15-minute call instead? I can ask the same questions and take notes, and then I will send you a proposal based on that conversation.
>
> Let me know a day and time that works for you, and I will send a calendar invite.
>
> If a call does not work either, I can send a rough ballpark estimate, but I want to be upfront: without understanding the scope, budget, and timeline, my estimate might not be accurate, and we may need to revise it once we discuss the details.
>
> What would you prefer?
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

If the client refuses the brief **and** refuses a call **and** still insists on a quote, that is a red flag. You can choose to send a cautious high-range estimate, or you can politely decline.

### Script 4 — When a client fills in the brief but leaves the budget blank

**Subject:** Re: [Project name] — Quick follow-up on budget

> Hi [Client name],
>
> Thanks for completing the brief. I have everything I need except the budget section, which was left blank.
>
> I completely understand if you are not sure what a fair budget is for this type of work. To help you, here are typical ranges for projects like yours:
>
> ✓ [Project type 1]: £[X]–£[Y]  
> ✓ [Project type 2]: £[X]–£[Y]  
> ✓ [Project type 3]: £[X]–£[Y]  
>
> Does any of those ranges feel realistic for you? If so, let me know which one, and I will tailor my proposal to fit within it.
>
> If your budget is below those ranges, I can suggest a phased approach or scaled-down scope that fits what you can afford.
>
> Looking forward to your reply.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

Budget clarity is non-negotiable. If the client still refuses to name a range, you can either walk away or send a proposal with your standard pricing and let them decide if it fits.

## Decision test — when a thin brief means walk away or raise the deposit

Not every brief is worth pursuing. Some briefs reveal the client is not ready, not serious, or not a good fit. Here is how to decide.

### Red flags in a project brief

✗ **No budget named** (even after you provide ranges)  
✗ **No decision-maker named** (client says "It depends on the board" but cannot name who is on the board or when they meet)  
✗ **Vague deliverables** (client writes "I need a brand" but cannot explain what that means)  
✗ **Unrealistic timeline** (client wants a 20-page website in 2 weeks)  
✗ **"Whatever you think" answers** to every question (client has not done any groundwork)  
✗ **Contradictory goals** (client wants "premium brand" but budget is £300)  

### What to do when you spot red flags

**Option 1: Educate the client**

If the client seems genuine but inexperienced, you can offer to help them refine their brief. Example:

> "Thanks for your brief. I noticed a few areas where I need more detail to give you an accurate quote. Would you be open to a 20-minute discovery call where I can walk you through what I need to know? No charge — I just want to make sure we are aligned before I propose anything."

**Option 2: Decline politely**

If the brief reveals the client is not a good fit (budget too low, expectations unrealistic, red flags everywhere), decline politely and quickly:

> "Thanks for sharing your brief. Based on the scope and timeline you have outlined, I do not think I am the right fit for this project. I would recommend [suggest an alternative: a junior freelancer, a template solution, or a scaled-down scope]. Best of luck with the project."

**Option 3: Proceed but raise the deposit**

If the brief is thin but the budget is strong and you want to take the risk, raise your deposit from 40% to 50% or 60%. A higher deposit protects you if the client turns out to be difficult or the scope expands.

## Project brief template variations — adapt for your discipline

The template above is general-purpose. Here are quick adaptations for specific freelance disciplines.

### For brand designers

Add questions about:  
✓ Competitors and market positioning  
✓ Existing brand perception (if rebranding)  
✓ Tone of voice and personality (friendly, corporate, bold, minimal)  
✓ Applications (print, digital, signage, packaging)  

### For web developers

Add questions about:  
✓ Hosting and domain (do they have it already?)  
✓ CMS preference (WordPress, Webflow, custom)  
✓ Integrations needed (payment gateway, CRM, booking system, API)  
✓ Content (will they provide it or do you need to write it?)  
✓ Accessibility and GDPR requirements  

### For copywriters and content strategists

Add questions about:  
✓ Existing content audit (do they have copy already or starting fresh?)  
✓ SEO goals (rankings, keywords, backlinks)  
✓ Tone and style (formal, conversational, technical, friendly)  
✓ Competitor content examples (what do they want to match or avoid?)  

### For consultants

Add questions about:  
✓ Current state and desired state (what is broken? what does success look like?)  
✓ Internal stakeholders and politics (who needs to be involved?)  
✓ Data and access (will you have access to the systems, financials, or customer data you need?)  
✓ Implementation support (are you advising only, or also implementing?)  

## Before you send your brief — pre-flight checklist

✓ Brief takes 10–15 minutes to complete (not 45 minutes)  
✓ Budget question is included and framed as "range" not "exact figure"  
✓ Every question has context or an example so the client knows what you are asking for  
✓ Tone is helpful and collaborative, not interrogative  
✓ You have tested the form yourself (if using Typeform or Google Forms) to ensure it works  
✓ You have stated how long it will take you to send a proposal after receiving the brief (e.g. "I will reply within 3 business days")  

## When a project brief is overkill — and when to skip it

Project briefs are not always necessary. Here is when you can skip them:

✓ **The enquiry is already detailed.** If the client sends a 3-page email outlining deliverables, budget, timeline, and decision-making process, you do not need a brief. Just confirm a few clarifications and send the proposal.  
✓ **The client is a repeat customer.** You already know how they work. Skip the brief and jump to the proposal.  
✓ **The project is tiny.** If someone asks for a single landing page or a logo refresh and the budget is under £500, a brief is overkill. Ask 3–5 clarifying questions over email and quote directly.  

Use your judgement. A brief is a tool, not a rule.
