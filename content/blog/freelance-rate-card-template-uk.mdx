## What a rate card is (and how it differs from a quote and a proposal)

A **rate card** is a reference price list for your services. It shows what you typically charge for common deliverables or time blocks (day, half-day, hour, or fixed project).

**Example:**

- Day rate (8 hours): £450
- Half-day (4 hours): £250
- Logo design (fixed): from £800

A rate card is **not** a quote. A quote prices a specific job. A rate card shows your general pricing so the client knows whether your rates fit their budget before they brief you on a project.

### Rate card vs quote vs proposal — comparison table

| | **Rate card** | **Quote** | **Proposal** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | Reference prices for many services | Fixed price for one job | Problem + approach + price |
| **Detail** | Service name, rate, what is included | Line items, total, payment terms | Scope, deliverables, timeline, terms, price |
| **When to send** | Prospect asks "what do you charge?" | Client asks "how much for this project?" | Client needs to see how you solve their problem |
| **Binding?** | No | Yes (once accepted) | Yes (once accepted) |
| **Covers many projects?** | Yes | No (one project) | No (one project) |

**When to use each:**

✓ **Rate card:** A marketing agency emails asking what you charge for web design. You send your rate card. They see your day rate is £500 and decide whether to brief you on a project.

✓ **Quote:** The agency briefs you on a 5-page website. You send a quote: £4,000 for design and build, with a breakdown and payment terms. They accept. You start work.

✓ **Proposal:** A startup asks you to redesign their product. You send a proposal: the problem (poor UX), your approach (user research, wireframes, design, testing), deliverables, 6-week timeline, £12,000 fee. They accept. You sign a contract.

For quote templates, see [quote template UK](/blog/quote-template-uk). For proposals, see [one-page freelance proposal template UK](/blog/one-page-freelance-proposal-template-uk) and [how to write a freelance proposal](/blog/how-to-write-a-freelance-proposal).

## What to put on a UK freelancer rate card — field checklist

A rate card is simpler than a quote or proposal, but it must still be clear enough to prevent misunderstandings.

### Required fields

✓ **Your name or business name**  
✓ **Contact details** (email, phone, or website)  
✓ **Currency** (£ for UK freelancers)  
✓ **VAT line** (if you are VAT registered, state "prices exclude VAT" and show your VAT number; if not registered, write "VAT not applicable" or leave it off)  
✓ **Services** with day rate, half-day rate, hourly rate, or fixed "from" prices  
✓ **What each price includes** (deliverables, revision rounds, turnaround time, file formats)  
✓ **Minimum booking or minimum fee** (e.g. "half-day minimum" or "minimum project £800")  
✓ **Rush or weekend uplift** (e.g. "urgent delivery within 48 hours: +50%")  
✓ **Deposit and payment terms** (e.g. "50% deposit, balance Net 14")  
✓ **Cancellation or kill fee** (e.g. "cancellation after project start: 50% fee")  
✓ **Expenses or travel** (e.g. "travel over 30 miles charged separately")  
✓ **Valid until date** (e.g. "valid until 31 December 2026")  
✓ **Final price note** (e.g. "final price confirmed in a written quote")

### Optional helpful fields

✓ **Industry or niche** (e.g. "rates for brand and web design")  
✓ **Examples of what you do** (e.g. "logos, websites, pitch decks")  
✓ **Response time** (e.g. "quotes sent within 24 hours")  
✓ **Testimonial or social proof** (one-line client quote)

**What you do NOT need:**

✗ Lengthy case studies (keep the rate card to one page)  
✗ Portfolio samples (link to your site instead)  
✗ Legal terms (those go in your quote, proposal, or contract)

## VAT wording for UK freelancers — how to show prices correctly

If you are VAT registered, you must add VAT to your prices. If you are not registered, you cannot add VAT.

### If you are VAT registered

**Show prices excluding VAT** and state that VAT will be added at the standard rate:

> All prices exclude VAT. VAT at 20% (standard rate) will be added to your invoice.  
> VAT number: GB 234 5678 90

**Example with VAT:**

- Day rate: £500 (excl. VAT)
- VAT at 20%: £100
- Total: £600

**Why show prices excluding VAT:**

Most UK business clients expect to see net prices (before VAT) because they can reclaim VAT. If you show VAT-inclusive prices, they may think your rate is higher than it actually is.

**Source:** [GOV.UK — VAT rates](https://www.gov.uk/vat-rates) confirms the standard rate is 20%.

### If you are NOT VAT registered

**Show prices without VAT** and optionally state:

> VAT not applicable (not VAT registered)

Or simply omit any VAT line.

**Example:**

- Day rate: £500
- Half-day: £275
- No VAT added.

**Do not charge VAT if you are not registered.** Charging VAT when you are not registered is illegal.

### When to register for VAT

You **must** register if your **taxable turnover** exceeds **£90,000** in a rolling 12-month period.

You **can** register voluntarily if your turnover is below that (useful if you pay VAT on expenses and want to reclaim it, or if your clients are mostly VAT-registered businesses and expect VAT invoices).

For full guidance on UK VAT registration, read [when to register for VAT UK freelancers](/blog/when-to-register-for-vat-uk-freelancers).

## How to set the numbers — worked £ example

Here is how to turn your target day rate into half-day, hourly, and fixed "from" prices.

**Scenario:**

You are a web designer. You want to earn **£500 per day** (8 hours). You need to calculate:

1. Half-day rate (4 hours)
2. Hourly rate (for small add-ons or revisions)
3. Fixed "from" price for a 5-page website

---

### Step 1: Start with your day rate

**Day rate:** £500 (8 hours)

This is your baseline. If a client books you for a full day, they pay £500.

---

### Step 2: Calculate half-day rate (not just half)

**Wrong:** £500 ÷ 2 = £250

**Why wrong:** A half-day (4 hours) requires the same setup, context-switching, and availability blocking as a full day. If you charge exactly half, you lose money because you cannot fill the other half-day with another client.

**Right:** Half-day = 55–60% of day rate

**Calculation:**

£500 × 0.60 = **£300 per half-day**

Or slightly lower if you want to be competitive:

£500 × 0.55 = **£275 per half-day**

**Use £275–£300 as your half-day rate.**

---

### Step 3: Calculate hourly rate (for small tasks, not for full projects)

**Wrong:** £500 ÷ 8 = £62.50 per hour

**Why wrong:** Hourly pricing for full projects invites scope creep. Clients think "one more hour won't hurt" and your £500 day turns into 12 hours for £750 (£62.50/hour × 12 = £750 — but you worked 50% more for only 50% more pay).

**Right:** Hourly rate = 125–150% of (day rate ÷ 8)

**Calculation:**

£500 ÷ 8 = £62.50 baseline hourly  
£62.50 × 1.30 (30% markup) = **£81 per hour**

Or round up to **£85 per hour**.

**Use £80–£85 as your hourly rate**, but only for:

- Small add-ons after a project is complete (e.g. "can you add one more page?")
- Revision rounds beyond what was included
- Client requests outside the original scope

**Do not offer hourly rates for full projects.** Use day/half-day or fixed prices instead.

---

### Step 4: Calculate fixed "from" price for a deliverable

**Example:** 5-page website design and build.

**Estimate your time:**

- Design: 2 days
- Build: 2 days
- Revisions, testing, handover: 1 day
- **Total: 5 days**

**Base price:**

5 days × £500 = £2,500

**Add a buffer for revisions and unknowns:**

£2,500 × 1.20 (20% buffer) = £3,000

Or add a fixed buffer:

£2,500 + £500 = £3,000

**Fixed price:** from £3,000

**Why "from" £3,000?**

The word "from" protects you if the client asks for features beyond the standard 5-page website (e.g. e-commerce, booking system, custom integrations). Your rate card shows the baseline. Your quote adjusts for complexity.

---

### Summary of your rate card pricing:

| Service | Calculation | Your rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Day rate** | Your target daily income | £500 |
| **Half-day** | Day rate × 0.55–0.60 | £275–£300 |
| **Hourly** | (Day rate ÷ 8) × 1.30 | £80–£85 |
| **5-page website** | 5 days + 20% buffer | from £3,000 |

## Benchmarks — UK industry rate cards you can cite

Some UK freelance fields publish recommended rate cards. These are not legally binding, but they give you a benchmark to cite when a client pushes back on your rates.

### Bectu rate cards (film, TV, and live events crew)

**Bectu** is the UK broadcasting union. Their freelance branches publish recommended going rates for film, TV, and live events crew.

**What they cover:**

- Camera operators, grips, sound, lighting, costume, props, runners, riggers, production accountants, assistant directors, editors, VFX, art department, location managers, and more.

**Example rates (from Bectu 2026 rate cards):**

These are illustrative examples. Always check the live Bectu rate card for your grade and region.

- Camera operator (drama): £400–£550 per day (depending on grade and budget)
- Sound recordist (drama): £450–£600 per day
- Runner (film): £100–£150 per day
- Production accountant (TV drama): £350–£500 per day

**How to use Bectu rate cards:**

If you work in film, TV, or live events, check whether Bectu publishes a rate card for your role:

[Bectu rate cards](https://bectu.org.uk/get-involved-in-the-union/ratecards/)

Bectu describes these as **"recommended going rates set by the relevant freelance branch(es), after consultation with members."**

**When a client says your rate is too high:**

Cite the Bectu rate card:

> "My day rate of £450 is in line with Bectu's recommended rate card for [your grade], which is £400–£550 per day for drama productions. This is the industry standard for experienced freelancers in the UK."

### Other UK industry rate bodies

**APA (Association of Photographers' Agents):** Publishes recommended rates for commercial photography assistants and crew.

**Gravy for the Brain:** A freelance TV and film production community that shares rate benchmarks in their Slack/Discord.

**NUJ (National Union of Journalists):** Publishes recommended minimum rates for journalists and writers (freelance article rates, day rates for shifts).

**SFIA (Skills Framework for the Information Age):** Not a rate card, but a skills framework used by some UK public sector and tech organisations to benchmark day rates for IT freelancers and contractors (e.g. "SFIA level 5 = £450–£600 per day").

**Method:**

Only cite a rate body if you have checked their current rates on their live website. Do not quote rates from outdated articles or forums. Bectu updates their rate cards annually, so check the date.

If your field does not have a published rate card, cite your own experience:

> "My day rate of £500 is based on 8 years' experience in brand and web design, and reflects the going rate for senior freelance designers in the UK. I consulted peers and checked industry surveys before setting this rate."

## Public vs private rate card — when to publish and when to keep it private

Should you publish your rate card on your website, or send it only when prospects ask?

### Publish your rate card on your website if:

✓ **You want to filter unqualified leads.** If your day rate is £500 and a prospect has a £200 budget, they will not contact you — saving you both time.

✓ **You work in a transparent pricing field.** Coaching, consulting, video editing, and some design niches are moving towards transparent pricing. Prospects expect to see rates before contacting you.

✓ **You have a clear value ladder.** E.g. Logo £800, brand identity £2,400, full rebrand £8,000. Publishing this helps prospects self-select into the right tier.

✓ **You are in demand and turning work away.** Publishing rates stops low-budget enquiries from filling your inbox.

**Where to publish:**

- A "Pricing" or "Rates" page on your website
- Your portfolio site (Behance, Dribble, or personal site)
- Your LinkedIn About section or Featured section

**Example wording:**

> **What I charge**  
> Day rate: £500 (8 hours, 2 revisions)  
> Half-day: £275 (4 hours, 1 revision)  
> Logo design: from £800  
> All prices exclude VAT.  
> Final price confirmed in a written quote.

---

### Keep your rate card private (send on request) if:

✓ **You price by project complexity, not time.** If your rates vary widely (a £1,200 project for a startup vs £12,000 for an enterprise client), a rate card may confuse or scare prospects.

✓ **Your rates depend on the client's budget.** Some freelancers adjust pricing based on client size (startup vs corporate). Publishing a rate card locks you into one price.

✓ **You compete on value, not price.** If your differentiator is "I solve complex problems", leading with price may attract price shoppers instead of value buyers.

✓ **You are building your portfolio and rates are still flexible.** Early-career freelancers often price projects individually until they establish a consistent rate.

**When to send your rate card:**

- A prospect emails: "What do you charge?"
- A lead asks for a "ballpark figure" before they brief you
- A recruiter asks for your day rate for a contract role

**How to send it:**

Attach as PDF or paste into the email:

> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks for asking. Here are my current rates:
>
> - Day rate: £500 (8 hours)
> - Half-day: £275 (4 hours)
> - Logo design: from £800
>
> These are reference rates. Once you brief me on your project, I will send a detailed quote with a fixed price.
>
> Let me know if you would like to discuss your project.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

---

### Only quote (no rate card at all) if:

✓ **Every project is custom.** If no two projects are the same (e.g. software architecture consulting, complex integrations), a rate card may not be useful.

✓ **You price by value, not time or deliverables.** Some freelancers charge based on the client's expected ROI (e.g. "I will increase your conversion rate by 20%, which is worth £50,000 to you, so my fee is £10,000").

**In this case:**

Skip the rate card. When a prospect asks "what do you charge?", reply:

> "My pricing depends on the scope and complexity. If you brief me on your project, I will send a quote within 24 hours."

Then send a quote or proposal with a fixed price for that specific job.

---

### Decision checklist:

✓ **Publish on your site** if you want to filter leads and you work in a transparent field  
✓ **Send on request** if you price by project and want to qualify leads before sharing rates  
✓ **Only quote** if every project is custom and value-based  

Most UK freelancers send rate cards on request rather than publishing openly. This lets you qualify the lead first (Do they have a real project? What is their budget?) before sharing your rates.

## When to update your rate card (and how to tell existing clients)

Your rates should increase as your experience, demand, and costs increase. But when should you update your rate card, and how do you handle existing clients?

### When to update your rate card:

✓ **Once a year** (January is common — gives you a fresh start and aligns with business planning)

✓ **After a run of projects where clients said yes immediately** without negotiation or pushback (a sign your rates are too low)

✓ **When you add a new service** or retire an old one

✓ **When your costs increase significantly** (software subscriptions, equipment, studio rent, childcare, inflation)

✓ **When you upskill** (e.g. you complete a course, get a certification, or add a valuable skill that commands higher rates)

✓ **When you are fully booked** and turning away work (increase rates to filter lower-value projects and free up time for higher-value clients)

### How much to increase:

**Small annual increase:** 3–5% per year to keep pace with inflation.

**Example:**

- Current day rate: £450
- 5% increase: £450 × 1.05 = £472.50
- Round to £475

**Significant increase:** 10–20% if you have added skills, built a strong portfolio, or moved from junior to mid-level or mid to senior.

**Example:**

- Current day rate: £350 (mid-level)
- Move to senior: £350 × 1.20 = £420
- Round to £425

**Do not double your rates overnight** unless you are pivoting to a different market (e.g. moving from startups to enterprise clients). Gradual increases are easier for existing clients to accept.

---

### How to tell existing clients about a rate increase:

**Rule:** New rates apply to **new projects only**, not to projects already quoted or in progress.

**When to tell them:**

✓ **30–60 days before the new rates take effect** (gives them time to book you at the old rate if they have a project in the pipeline)

✓ **When they ask for a new quote** (mention the rate increase in the quote email)

✓ **In your end-of-year update email** (December or January — a good time to remind clients you exist and mention new rates)

**Email template:**

> Hi [Client name],
>
> I wanted to give you advance notice that my rates are increasing from 1 January 2027.
>
> **New rates:**
>
> - Day rate: £500 (currently £450)
> - Half-day: £275 (currently £250)
> - Logo design: from £850 (currently from £800)
>

>

>
> Thank you for your continued support. I look forward to working with you again.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

**Why this works:**

✓ You give them notice (not a surprise)  
✓ You offer a grace period (book before the deadline at the old rate)  
✓ You explain that current projects are unaffected (no mid-project surprises)  
✓ You frame it positively ("continued support", "looking forward")

**What if a long-term client pushes back?**

Offer to hold the old rate for them **for one more project** or **for 6 months**, then move to the new rate after that. This rewards loyalty without locking you into old rates forever.

**Example:**

> "I understand you have been a long-term client. I am happy to hold the current rate of £450/day for your next project, and then move to the new rate of £500/day for projects after that."

### Do not apologise for increasing your rates

Your rates reflect your value. As you gain experience, your work improves and your time becomes more valuable. Clients who respect your work will accept a reasonable increase. Clients who balk at a 5–10% rise after a year are price shoppers who will leave eventually anyway.

## Common mistakes that turn rate cards into disputes

**No validity date.** A prospect from 2024 digs up your old rate card and expects you to honour it in 2026. Always state "valid until [date]".

**Hourly rate only, no minimum.** A client books you for "one hour" to tweak a logo. You spend 20 minutes on the task and 40 minutes on setup, file prep, and sending the revised file. Your £80 hourly rate just became £80 for an hour of admin and 20 minutes of real work. Always add a minimum: "half-day minimum" or "minimum fee £200".

**No "what's included" line.** A client books your day rate and expects unlimited revisions, same-day delivery, and a Zoom call to walk them through the files. Your rate card said "£500 per day" but not what that day includes. State: "8 hours work, 2 revisions, delivered within 5 days".

**Rush work not priced separately.** A client emails Friday afternoon asking for delivery Monday morning. You work the weekend. Your rate card did not mention rush fees. Add: "urgent delivery within 48 hours: +50%" or "weekend work: +30%".

**VAT confusion.** You are VAT registered but your rate card shows £500 without saying "excl. VAT". Client thinks £500 is the total. You invoice £600 (£500 + £100 VAT). Client is confused and delays payment. Always state: "prices exclude VAT" or "plus VAT at 20%".

**Expenses not clarified.** You design a brand identity. Client assumes stock fonts are included. You buy a £200 font licence and add it to the invoice. Client refuses to pay because it was not in the rate card. State: "stock images, fonts, and third-party services charged separately (quoted in advance)".

**No "final price confirmed in a quote" note.** A client treats your rate card as a binding quote. They book you for a 5-page website at "from £3,000" and expect that exact price, even though their brief now includes e-commerce, a blog, and a booking system. Add: "final price confirmed in a written quote" or "rates are subject to project scope".

**Copying someone else's rate card without adjusting for your experience or location.** You find a rate card online for a "senior brand designer — £600/day" and copy it. You are mid-level with 3 years' experience. Clients sense the mismatch and push back. Set rates based on **your** experience, location, niche, and market research (ask peers, check Bectu or other benchmarks, survey past clients).

## FAQ

### What is a rate card for freelancers?

A rate card is a one-page reference sheet showing your prices for common services — day rate, half-day, hourly, or fixed deliverables. It is sent to prospects who ask "what do you charge?" before you quote a specific project. It differs from a quote (which prices one job) and a proposal (which includes scope, approach, and price).

### Should I publish my rate card on my website?

It depends. Publish if: you want to filter unqualified leads, you work in a transparent field (coaching, video editing), or you have a clear value ladder. Keep it private if: you price by project complexity, your rates vary by client budget, or you compete on value not price. Most UK freelancers send rate cards on request rather than publishing openly.

### How do I show VAT on a UK rate card?

If you are VAT registered, show prices excluding VAT and state "plus VAT at 20%" or list your VAT number. If you are not VAT registered, show prices without VAT and write "VAT not applicable" or "Not VAT registered". You must register for VAT if your taxable turnover exceeds £90,000 in 12 months.

### What should I include on a freelance rate card?

Include: your name and contact details, currency (£ for UK), services with day/half-day/hourly/fixed rates, what each price includes (deliverables, revisions, turnaround), minimum booking or fee, rush/weekend uplift, deposit and payment terms, cancellation or kill fee, expenses policy, valid-until date, and a note that final prices are confirmed in a written quote.

### How often should I update my rate card?

Update once a year (January is common), after a run of projects where clients said yes without negotiation (a sign your prices are too low), when you add a new service, or when your costs increase significantly. Tell existing clients about increases 30–60 days in advance and only apply new rates to new projects.

### What is the difference between a rate card, a quote, and a proposal?

A rate card shows reference prices for many services. A quote prices one specific job with a fixed total. A proposal includes the problem, your approach, deliverables, timeline, and price. Send a rate card when a prospect asks "what do you charge generally". Send a quote when they ask "how much for this project". Send a proposal when they need to see how you will solve their problem.
