Looking for a **freelance invoice template** that gets you paid without follow-up questions? This guide covers GOV.UK invoice requirements, three copy-paste GBP examples, VAT guidance, and your late payment rights under UK law.

A clean invoice does three things: it tells the client exactly what they owe, it gives finance all the details they need to process payment, and it protects you if payment goes missing.

This is not about fancy design. It is about completeness. The invoices below are built for UK freelancers — designers, developers, writers, consultants, and small studios working with UK clients or invoicing in GBP.

**Disclaimer:** This guide is for general education. It is not legal, tax, or accounting advice. Invoice and VAT rules vary by business structure and jurisdiction. When in doubt, consult an accountant.

## What GOV.UK says your invoice must include

UK invoice requirements come from HMRC guidance and vary slightly depending on whether you are VAT registered. Here is what your invoice needs to be legally compliant.

### For all UK invoices

Every invoice you send should include:

✓ **A unique identification number** — never reuse a number  
✓ **Your business name and address** — the name clients know you by, plus a UK address  
✓ **Your contact information** — email or phone  
✓ **The customer's name and address**  
✓ **A clear description** of the goods or services supplied  
✓ **The date of supply** (when the work was delivered or the service completed)  
✓ **The invoice date** (when the invoice was issued)  
✓ **The amount owed** — broken down by line item, then totalled  
✓ **VAT information** (if you are VAT registered — more below)

### If you are a sole trader

Show your full name and any business name you use. If you use a business name, your full name must also appear somewhere on the invoice.

You must include an address where legal documents can be delivered to you.

### If you are a limited company

Show the full registered company name exactly as it appears on your certificate of incorporation.

If you include any director names on the invoice, you must list all directors.

Your company registration number can be helpful for larger clients, though it is not always mandatory.

### If you are VAT registered

Your invoice must show:

✓ Your VAT registration number  
✓ The rate of VAT charged (usually 20% standard rate)  
✓ The net amount, the VAT amount, and the gross total separately  
✓ A statement that the invoice is a VAT invoice

If you are **not VAT registered**, do not charge VAT. You can write "VAT not applicable" or "Not VAT registered" if a client asks, but it is not required.

**VAT registration threshold:** As of 1 September 2026, you must register for VAT if your taxable turnover exceeds **£90,000** in a rolling 12-month period, or if you expect it to exceed that threshold in the next 30 days. The standard VAT rate is **20%**.

**Source:** [GOV.UK — Invoicing and taking payment from customers](https://www.gov.uk/invoicing-and-taking-payment-from-customers/invoices-what-they-must-include) and [GOV.UK — VAT registration](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat)

## UK payment details to include

Put your payment details directly on the invoice. Do not make finance teams hunt for them in a follow-up email.

**For UK bank transfers:**

✓ Your bank account name (should match your invoice name)  
✓ Sort code  
✓ Account number  

**If you invoice clients abroad:**

✓ IBAN  
✓ BIC/SWIFT code  
✓ Bank name and address  

**For card payments:**

Include a Stripe, PayPal, or Wise payment link directly on the invoice. Some clients prefer to pay by card, especially for smaller amounts.

Make it easy. The fewer clicks between the invoice and the payment, the faster you get paid.

## Payment terms that protect you

A due date is not optional. It sets the boundary. Without it, "I'll pay you soon" becomes "I'll pay you whenever."

Here are the most common payment terms UK freelancers use:

**Due on receipt** — payment is expected as soon as the invoice is issued. Works well for small jobs, trusted clients, or retainers billed at the start of the month.

**Net 7** — payment due within 7 calendar days. A good default for most project invoices under £3,000. Keeps cash flowing and signals you track payments.

**Net 14** — payment due within 14 days. Common with larger brands and slower finance teams. Still reasonable for freelancers.

**Net 30** — payment due within 30 days. Standard in corporate environments. Fine if your cashflow can handle the wait. Do not go longer unless the client is worth it.

**Calendar due date** — always show the actual date. "Payment due 15 September 2026" is clearer than "Net 14" alone. Do both if you want, but the calendar date is the one that counts.

### Deposits and milestone invoicing

Take a **40–50% deposit** before starting work on any project over £1,000. The deposit is non-refundable once work begins. This is standard practice and protects both sides.

For longer projects, invoice at milestones instead of waiting until the end. Smaller invoices are easier for clients to approve and easier for you to chase if something goes wrong.

### Late fees and statutory interest

If you want to charge a late fee, write it into your contract **before** the project starts, and repeat the same wording on the invoice. Do not surprise clients with fees they never agreed to.

**UK statutory rights:** Under the **Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998**, you have the right to charge interest and debt recovery costs when a business client (B2B) pays late. You do not need to write this into your contract — the law gives you that right automatically.

#### How statutory interest works

The interest rate is the **Bank of England base rate plus 8%**.

As of 1 September 2026, the Bank of England base rate is **3.75%** (held since 30 July 2026; next decision scheduled for 17 September 2026). That means the current statutory rate is **11.75% per year**, or roughly **0.98% per month**.

**Worked example:**  
Invoice: £1,900  
Due date: 15 September 2026  
Paid: 15 October 2026 (30 days late)  
Interest (30 days at 11.75% p.a.): approximately **£18.40**

#### Debt recovery costs

You can also claim a fixed fee for chasing the debt:

✓ **£40** for debts under £1,000  
✓ **£70** for debts between £1,000 and £9,999.99  
✓ **£100** for debts over £10,000  

So on that late £1,900 invoice, you could claim:

- Original invoice: £1,900  
- Statutory interest (30 days): £18.40  
- Debt recovery costs: £70  
- **Total due: £1,988.40**

#### Important notes

Statutory interest applies to **commercial contracts only** (business to business). Different rules apply to consumer contracts (business to consumer).

You cannot claim statutory interest if your contract already specifies a different interest rate. Your contract terms take priority.

This is a high-level summary. For detailed guidance, see [GOV.UK — Late commercial payments: charging interest and debt recovery](https://www.gov.uk/late-commercial-payments-interest-debt-recovery). This guide is not legal advice.

## Mistakes that delay payment

**No calendar due date.** "Net 14" is ambiguous. "Due 15 September 2026" is not. Always show the actual date.

**Missing sort code or account number.** If finance has to email you for bank details, payment is delayed by at least a day. Put them on the invoice.

**Vague line items.** "Consulting" or "Design work" tells finance nothing. "Brand logo redesign and 10-page brand guideline document" tells them everything.

**Wrong billing contact.** The person you worked with is not always the person who approves invoices. Ask for the billing contact and accounts payable email before you send.

**No purchase order when one was required.** Large companies and public sector clients often need a PO number to process payment. Ask at the start of the project. Add it to the invoice.

**Currency confusion.** If you invoice in GBP, write "£1,900 GBP" on the total line. Do not assume.

**Charging VAT when you are not registered.** Only charge VAT if you are VAT registered. If you are not, do not invent a VAT charge. You will confuse the client and create a mess with HMRC.

**Sending before the work is done.** Invoice when the milestone is complete, unless your contract says otherwise. Clients do not pay for promises.

**No follow-up.** If the invoice is unpaid on the day after the due date, send a polite reminder. The freelancers who get paid consistently are the ones who chase calmly and persistently.

## Before you send — final checklist

Walk this list once before you hit send. Two minutes now saves two weeks of chasing.

✓ Invoice number is unique and logged  
✓ Client name and billing email are correct  
✓ Line items match the agreed scope or contract  
✓ Total matches the contract or change order  
✓ Due date is a real calendar date  
✓ Sort code and account number are visible on the invoice  
✓ VAT is only charged if you are registered  
✓ PO number is included if the client provided one  
✓ PDF is clean and readable on mobile  
✓ You saved a copy in your records folder  
✓ You logged the expected payment date in your calendar  
✓ You are ready to send a reminder if they miss the due date

## Hourly vs fixed vs retainer invoices

**Hourly invoices** need a time period. Show the dates you worked (e.g. "Service period: 19 August – 30 August 2026"). Break down the hours by task or day if that helps. Show your hourly rate clearly. Round consistently — most consultants use quarter-hours or half-hours.

**Fixed-price invoices** work best with milestones. Do not bill "50% complete" if the client cannot see what 50% means. Bill the phase: "Homepage design and development," "User research and wireframes," "Final delivery and handoff." Name the deliverable. Clients approve faster when the milestone matches the contract.

**Retainer invoices** need a service month. State what is included in the retainer. State what counts as additional work. Put the extra line items separately so the client understands what they are paying for. Retainers are easiest to invoice at the start of the month with "Due on receipt" terms.

**Mixed invoices** are fine. You can put fixed-price work and hourly work on the same invoice. Keep them in separate sections so the maths is obvious.

## Send it, track it, chase it

A good invoice does not guarantee fast payment. But it removes friction. It answers the questions finance would ask before they ask them.

That means fewer follow-up emails, fewer delays, and less stress.

Send the invoice as a PDF. Keep a copy. Log the due date. Set a reminder for the day after. If they miss the deadline, send a polite nudge. Then a firmer one. Then escalate if needed.

Getting paid is a skill. A clean invoice is the first tool in that skill.
