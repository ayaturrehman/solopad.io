## Why UK freelancers undersell (and how to stop)

Most freelancers set rates by copying what they see online. But freelance rate benchmarks rarely account for:

✓ Your location (London vs Newcastle)  
✓ Your niche (fintech vs local bakery)  
✓ Your speed (2 days vs 2 weeks for the same deliverable)  
✓ Your business costs (software, insurance, equipment, tax)  
✓ Your unbilled time (proposals, admin, chasing invoices)  

A rate that works for a junior designer in Manchester will not work for a senior developer in London. A rate calculator that assumes 1,800 billable hours will not work if you only bill 900 hours.

**Why freelancers quote too low:**

✓ They compare themselves to employed salaries (forgetting that employed salaries do not include employer NI, pension, sick pay, holiday pay, equipment, or software)  
✓ They use their old employed hourly rate (dividing salary by 2,080 hours — but freelancers rarely bill 2,080 hours)  
✓ They see competitor websites and guess those rates are market rate (but many competitors also undercharge)  
✓ They fear the client will say no (so they quote low "just to get the work")  

**The fix:** Set your rate based on your own costs, target income, and billable capacity — not on what you think others charge.

## Step 1 — Calculate your baseline annual income target

Before you set an hourly or day rate, work out **how much money you need to earn per year** to live, save, and grow your business.

### UK freelancer income formula

Your annual income target should cover:

1. **Living costs** — rent/mortgage, bills, food, transport, personal expenses  
2. **Taxes** — Income Tax, National Insurance (Class 2 and Class 4), VAT if registered  
3. **Business costs** — software, equipment, insurance, training, marketing, accountant fees  
4. **Savings and profit** — emergency fund, pension, business reinvestment, personal savings  

**Worked example — UK freelancer baseline income target:**

| Item | Annual Amount (GBP) |
| --- | --- |
| Living costs (after tax) | £28,000 |
| Income Tax and NI (approx 25–30% of gross) | £11,000 |
| Business costs (software, insurance, equipment, training) | £4,000 |
| Savings and profit (pension, emergency fund, reinvestment) | £7,000 |
| **Total annual income target** | **£50,000** |

This is your **gross income target** — the total you need to invoice per year to cover all your costs and pay yourself a decent living.

**Tip:** If you are newly self-employed, use your old employed salary as a starting point for living costs. Then add 30–40% to cover taxes, business costs, and savings. If you earned £30,000 employed, target £40,000–45,000 freelance to maintain the same take-home.

### How to calculate your take-home after tax and NI

Use HMRC's online calculator to estimate Income Tax and National Insurance: [https://www.gov.uk/estimate-income-tax](https://www.gov.uk/estimate-income-tax)

**Quick estimate for UK freelancers (2026/27 tax year):**

- £20,000 gross → approx £18,500 take-home (8% tax + NI)  
- £35,000 gross → approx £29,800 take-home (15% tax + NI)  
- £50,000 gross → approx £39,000 take-home (22% tax + NI)  
- £75,000 gross → approx £55,000 take-home (27% tax + NI)  

These are rough estimates. Tax depends on your personal allowance, pension contributions, and whether you operate as a sole trader or limited company. Check with an accountant if your income is over £50,000 or you plan to go limited.

## Step 2 — Calculate your billable hours per year

Freelancers do not work 2,080 hours per year (52 weeks × 40 hours). Most UK freelancers bill **900 to 1,200 hours per year**.

Why the difference? Because freelancers spend significant time on **unbilled work**:

✓ Writing proposals and quotes  
✓ Client calls and project kickoffs  
✓ Admin (invoicing, bookkeeping, emails)  
✓ Marketing and business development (website, LinkedIn, networking)  
✓ Learning and skills development  
✓ Sick days, holiday, and downtime between projects  

**Realistic billable hours for UK freelancers:**

| Scenario | Billable Hours per Year |
| --- | --- |
| Part-time freelancer (2–3 days/week) | 400–600 hours |
| Full-time freelancer, first year (building client base) | 800–1,000 hours |
| Established freelancer, steady pipeline | 1,000–1,200 hours |
| Senior freelancer, productised services | 1,200–1,400 hours |
| Very high-demand specialist | 1,400–1,600 hours |

**How to estimate your billable hours:**

1. Assume **48 working weeks per year** (52 weeks minus 4 weeks holiday/sick/downtime)  
2. Multiply by **hours per week you plan to work** (e.g. 40 hours)  
3. Multiply by **billable percentage** (typically 50–70%)  

**Example:**

- 48 weeks × 40 hours = 1,920 total hours  
- 1,920 hours × 60% billable = **1,152 billable hours**  

**Tip:** Track your time for 3 months using a time tracker (Toggl, Clockify, or SoloPad). Calculate what percentage of your time is actually billable vs admin/proposals/marketing. Use that percentage to estimate your annual billable hours.

## Step 3 — Calculate your hourly rate (with worked GBP example)

Once you know your annual income target and billable hours, your hourly rate formula is simple:

**Hourly Rate = Annual Income Target ÷ Billable Hours per Year**

### Worked example — UK freelancer hourly rate

**Income target:** £50,000  
**Billable hours:** 1,000 hours  
**Calculation:** £50,000 ÷ 1,000 = **£50/hour**

But this is your **break-even rate**. You need a buffer for:

✓ Unexpected business costs  
✓ Client payment delays (you invoice today, get paid in 30–60 days)  
✓ Gaps between projects (not every week is fully booked)  

Add **30–50%** to your break-even rate to create a **target rate**:

- £50/hour × 1.3 = **£65/hour** (conservative buffer)  
- £50/hour × 1.5 = **£75/hour** (comfortable buffer)  

**Final hourly rate:** £65–75/hour

**Tip:** Round your rate to a clean number. £67/hour feels fiddly. £70/hour is clearer and easier to quote.

## Step 4 — Calculate your day rate (from hourly rate)

A **day rate** is a flat fee for a full working day (typically 7–8 hours).

Day rates are popular for:

✓ Longer projects (1–4 weeks)  
✓ Consulting, strategy, or workshop facilitation  
✓ Design and development sprints  
✓ Situations where tracking exact hours feels petty  

### Day rate formula

**Day Rate = Hourly Rate × 6 to 8 hours**

Why not always 8 hours? Because day rates include breaks, context switching, and client calls. A day rate compensates you for blocking a full day, not for billing every minute.

**Worked example — UK freelancer day rate:**

- Hourly rate: £70/hour  
- Day rate (7 hours): £70 × 7 = **£490/day**  
- Day rate (8 hours): £70 × 8 = **£560/day**  

Round to a clean number: **£500/day**

## Step 5 — Three-tier package pricing (good, better, best)

Hourly and day rates work well for unpredictable scope or time-based work. But for **defined deliverables** (website, brand identity, content package), **project rates** (packages) work better.

Packages let you:

✓ Price based on value, not time (a fast designer can charge the same as a slow designer)  
✓ Avoid scope creep (the package defines what is included)  
✓ Give clients choice (three tiers anchor the middle option as best value)  

### How to build three-tier pricing

**Tier 1 — Starter/Essential** (smallest viable deliverable)

✓ Fixed scope, no customisation  
✓ One round of revisions  
✓ Clear delivery timeline (e.g. 5 working days)  
✓ 50–60% of your mid-tier price  

**Tier 2 — Standard/Growth** (your recommended option)

✓ More scope, some customisation  
✓ Two rounds of revisions  
✓ Longer timeline or faster turnaround  
✓ Your base price for a typical project  

**Tier 3 — Premium/Pro** (highest value, most customisation)

✓ Full scope, bespoke deliverables  
✓ Three rounds of revisions  
✓ Priority support, faster delivery, extras  
✓ 150–200% of your mid-tier price  

**Why three tiers work:** Most clients choose the middle tier. The high tier makes the middle tier look reasonable. The low tier filters out clients who cannot afford you.

### Worked example — UK freelance web designer three-tier pricing

**Tier 1 — Starter Website Package (£1,800)**

✓ 3-page website (Home, About, Contact)  
✓ Template-based design (choose from 5 pre-designed themes)  
✓ Mobile responsive  
✓ Contact form  
✓ 1 round of revisions  
✓ Delivered in 7 working days  

**Tier 2 — Growth Website Package (£3,200)**

✓ 5-page website (Home, About, Services, Portfolio/Case Studies, Contact)  
✓ Custom design (brand colours, fonts, layout)  
✓ Mobile responsive  
✓ Contact form + newsletter signup  
✓ 2 rounds of revisions  
✓ Basic SEO setup (meta titles, descriptions)  
✓ Delivered in 14 working days  

**Tier 3 — Premium Website Package (£5,500)**

✓ 8-page website (Home, About, Services, Portfolio, Blog, FAQ, Resources, Contact)  
✓ Bespoke design (full brand expression)  
✓ Mobile responsive  
✓ Contact form + newsletter signup + lead magnet download  
✓ 3 rounds of revisions  
✓ Advanced SEO (schema markup, sitemap, performance optimisation)  
✓ Content support (help writing or editing page copy)  
✓ Priority delivery in 10 working days  

**How the tiers work:**

- Budget-conscious clients pick Starter (£1,800)  
- Most clients pick Growth (£3,200) — best value  
- High-budget clients or those with urgent deadlines pick Premium (£5,500)  

**Tip:** Present all three tiers at once in your proposal. Do not ask "What is your budget?" and then show one tier. Let the client choose.

### Worked example — UK freelance content writer three-tier pricing

**Tier 1 — Essential Content Package (£900)**

✓ 3 blog posts (1,200 words each)  
✓ Topic research and keyword optimisation  
✓ 1 round of revisions per post  
✓ Delivered over 10 working days  

**Tier 2 — Content Growth Package (£1,600)**

✓ 6 blog posts (1,200–1,500 words each)  
✓ Topic research, keyword optimisation, and meta descriptions  
✓ 2 rounds of revisions per post  
✓ Internal linking suggestions  
✓ Delivered over 15 working days  

**Tier 3 — Content Authority Package (£2,800)**

✓ 10 blog posts (1,500–2,000 words each)  
✓ Topic research, keyword optimisation, and meta descriptions  
✓ 2 rounds of revisions per post  
✓ Internal linking + content hub structure  
✓ Social media captions (LinkedIn/Twitter) for each post  
✓ Delivered over 20 working days  

**Tip:** Use packages for productised services (repeatable deliverables with clear scope). Use hourly or day rates for custom or exploratory work where scope is uncertain.

## Step 6 — How to put your rate in a freelance proposal (without sounding too expensive)

Many freelancers bury their rate at the end of a proposal, hoping the client will not notice. This makes you look unconfident and invites negotiation.

Instead, present your rate clearly, confidently, and **anchored by value**.

### Proposal rate wording template (single rate)

**Investment:**

> This project is **£3,200** (paid in two instalments: 50% upfront, 50% on delivery).
>
> **What is included:**
>
> ✓ Custom 5-page website design (Home, About, Services, Portfolio, Contact)  
> ✓ Mobile responsive, fast loading, SEO-optimised  
> ✓ Contact form + newsletter signup integration  
> ✓ 2 rounds of revisions  
> ✓ 2 weeks of post-launch support  
> ✓ Delivered in 14 working days  

**Why this wording works:**

✓ "Investment" frames the cost as value, not expense  
✓ Bullet points show exactly what they get  
✓ Clear payment terms and timeline  
✓ Confident tone (not "I charge" or "My rate is")  

### Proposal rate wording template (three tiers)

Present all three tiers in a table so the client can compare:

| | **Starter** | **Growth** | **Premium** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pages** | 3 pages | 5 pages | 8 pages |
| **Design** | Template-based | Custom design | Bespoke design |
| **Revisions** | 1 round | 2 rounds | 3 rounds |
| **SEO** | Basic | Basic | Advanced |
| **Delivery** | 7 days | 14 days | 10 days (priority) |
| **Investment** | **£1,800** | **£3,200** | **£5,500** |

> **My recommendation:** The **Growth Package (£3,200)** gives you the best balance of custom design, flexibility, and value. Most clients choose this option.
>
> Payment terms: 50% deposit, 50% on delivery.

**Why this wording works:**

✓ Table makes it easy to compare  
✓ Your recommendation guides the client to the mid-tier  
✓ Client feels empowered to choose, not pressured  

**Tip:** Always include payment terms in the proposal. If you invoice on completion but the client expects to pay in 30 days, you have a cashflow problem. Make payment terms clear upfront.

## Step 7 — When and how to raise your freelance rates

Your costs go up. Your skills improve. Your calendar fills. Your rates should increase too.

Most UK freelancers **under-raise rates** — they keep the same rate for 2–3 years, eroding their real income as inflation rises.

**When to raise your rates:**

✓ Your calendar is **80%+ booked** (you are turning down work)  
✓ You have not raised rates in **6–12 months**  
✓ You gained a new skill, credential, or specialisation (new software, certification, niche pivot)  
✓ Inflation eroded your real income (UK CPI inflation is 2–4% per year — your rate should track this)  
✓ You consistently deliver results faster or better than competitors  

**How much to raise:**

✓ **5–10% increase** — standard annual adjustment for inflation and experience growth  
✓ **10–15% increase** — you added new services, skills, or your demand is high  
✓ **20–30% increase** — you pivoted to a higher-value niche or productised your service  

**Who to raise rates for:**

✓ **New clients:** Increase immediately (they do not know your old rate)  
✓ **Existing clients:** Notify 30–60 days before the increase takes effect  

### Raise-rates email template (existing clients)

**Subject:** Rate update — effective [Date]

> Hi [Client Name],
>
> I wanted to give you advance notice that my rates will be increasing from **[Old Rate]** to **[New Rate]** from **[Date]** (60 days from today).
>
> I have held my rates steady for the past [X months/years], and during that time I have [grown my skills / expanded my services / invested in new tools / increased my specialisation in [niche]].
>
> I value working with you and wanted to ensure you had plenty of time to plan for this change.
>
> **What this means for current projects:**
>
> - Any work booked or invoiced before [Date] will be billed at the old rate (£[Old Rate])  
> - Any new work starting after [Date] will be billed at the new rate (£[New Rate])  
>
> If you have any upcoming projects you would like to book before the rate change, let me know by [Deadline] and I will secure your slot at the current rate.
>
> Thanks for your continued trust and collaboration.
>
> Best regards,  
> [Your Name]

### Worked example — rate increase email with GBP amounts

**Subject:** Rate update — effective 1 November 2026

> Hi Sophie,
>
> I wanted to give you advance notice that my day rate will be increasing from **£450/day** to **£500/day** from **1 November 2026** (60 days from today).
>

>

>
> - Any work booked or invoiced before 1 November will be billed at £450/day  
> - Any new work starting after 1 November will be billed at £500/day  
>
> If you have any upcoming projects you would like to book before the rate change, let me know by 30 September and I will secure your slot at the current rate.
>
> Thanks for your continued trust and collaboration.
>
> Best regards,

**Why this wording works:**

✓ Clear, professional, unapologetic  
✓ Gives client 60 days to plan (or book at old rate)  
✓ Explains why (skills, time since last increase)  
✓ Confirms current projects are unaffected  

**What if the client says no or ghosts?** That is okay. Clients who cannot afford a 10–15% increase after 12–18 months are not your long-term clients. Focus on attracting new clients at your new rate.

## What if a client says your rate is too high?

This happens. Here is how to handle it without immediately discounting.

### Step 1 — Ask about their budget

> "I appreciate that. What budget did you have in mind for this project?"

If their budget is 20–30% below your rate, you can adjust scope. If it is 50%+ below, they cannot afford you — politely decline.

### Step 2 — Offer a smaller scope (not a discount)

> "My day rate is £500/day. If your budget is £1,500, I can offer a 3-day scope instead of 5 days. That would cover [reduced deliverable list]."

**Why this works:** You maintain your rate. The client gets something they can afford. You do not train them to expect discounts.

### Step 3 — Suggest a phased approach

> "Let's start with a smaller project (£1,200) to see if we work well together. If it goes well, we can discuss a larger engagement later."

**Why this works:** You reduce the client's risk. They get to test you before committing to a big budget. You still charge your full rate.

### Step 4 — Politely decline

If the client cannot afford you and will not adjust scope or budget:

> "I understand budget is tight. Unfortunately, my rates are fixed to ensure I can deliver the quality you need. I recommend reaching out to [Name of junior freelancer or agency in your network] who may be a better fit for your budget."

**Why this works:** You stay professional. You do not waste time negotiating. You refer them to someone who can help. You protect your rate.

**Tip:** If you discount once, the client will expect discounts forever. Protect your rate. Adjust scope instead.

## Common rate-setting mistakes UK freelancers make

**Charging the same rate for all clients.** High-value clients (corporates, agencies with big budgets, urgent deadlines) can afford more. Low-budget clients (startups, local businesses, charities) cannot. Adjust your rate based on the client's ability to pay and the project value.

**Not tracking time.** You think you spent 10 hours on a project. You actually spent 18 hours. Your effective rate drops from £70/hour to £39/hour. Track your time for 3 months to see your real billable percentage.

**Forgetting to account for unbilled time.** You quote £1,000 for a project. You spend 14 hours on the project, 3 hours on the proposal, 2 hours on revisions, and 1 hour invoicing and chasing payment. 20 hours total. Your effective rate: £50/hour, not £71/hour.

**Not raising rates for years.** UK inflation averages 2–4% per year. If you do not raise your rates, you are earning less in real terms. Raise rates annually for new clients, and every 18–24 months for existing clients.

**Quoting too fast.** The client asks "What do you charge?" and you panic and blurt out a number. Take time to think. Say: "Let me review the brief and send you a detailed quote by [Date]." This gives you time to calculate scope, effort, and value.

**Using hourly rates for fixed-scope projects.** Hourly rates punish fast workers. If you can deliver a logo in 5 hours but a junior designer takes 15 hours, why should you earn less? For fixed-scope work, use project rates (packages).

**Not offering three tiers.** If you only offer one price, clients cannot compare. They compare your one price to your competitor's one price. Three tiers give clients choice and make your middle tier look like best value.
