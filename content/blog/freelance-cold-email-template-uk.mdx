## What cold email is (and what it is not)

A **cold email** is an unsolicited business email sent to a specific person you have no prior relationship with, offering a service or starting a conversation that could lead to work.

**Cold email is:**

✓ Targeted (you researched the recipient and know why your service fits)  
✓ Relevant (you reference their work, product, or recent activity)  
✓ Short (under 100 words, ideally 50–75)  
✓ Specific (you state what you do and what you offer, not vague "let's collaborate")  
✓ Low-pressure (you ask for permission to share an example or brief, not a 30-minute call)  

**Cold email is NOT:**

✗ A mass-blasted template sent to 500 people  
✗ A sales pitch disguised as a partnership opportunity  
✗ A request for a 30-minute "pick your brain" call  
✗ An attachment-heavy portfolio dump  
✗ Spam (which is illegal under UK PECR)  

Cold email is a **door-opener**, not a sales letter. Your goal is to get a reply, not close the deal in the first email.

## Decision tree — when to use cold email (and when NOT to)

Cold email is not always the right channel. Before you send 50 cold emails, check this decision tree.

### Use cold email when:

✓ You have **no warm leads** and your pipeline is empty  
✓ You have **researched the recipient** and can explain why your service fits their business  
✓ The recipient has a **clear need** (e.g. outdated website, active hiring, recent product launch)  
✓ You can **prove value quickly** (case study, sample, or specific suggestion)  
✓ You are targeting **B2B contacts** at companies with 5+ employees (not personal Gmail addresses)  

### Do NOT use cold email when:

✗ You have **warm leads or referrals available** (warm channels convert 5–10× better)  
✗ You are targeting **individual consumers** with personal email addresses (PECR restrictions apply)  
✗ You have **no research** and are copy-pasting the same template to everyone  
✗ The recipient is **swamped with cold pitches** (e.g. startup founders, journalists, influencers)  
✗ You are asking for **free advice, intros, or unpaid work** instead of offering paid value  

**Better alternatives to cold email:**

1. **Warm intros:** Ask your network for introductions to target clients  
2. **Referrals:** Incentivise past clients to refer you (10–20% finder's fee or gift)  
3. **Content marketing:** Publish case studies or how-to guides that attract inbound leads  
4. **LinkedIn outreach:** Comment on their posts, DM after engagement (warmer than cold email)  
5. **Local networking:** Attend industry events, co-working spaces, or trade meetups  

If you have any of those options available, use them first. Cold email is for filling gaps, not replacing referrals.

## Anatomy of a cold email that gets replies

Most cold emails fail because they are too long, too vague, or too focused on the sender. Here is the structure that works.

### Line 1: Proof you researched (about them, not you)

Open with one line that proves you know who they are and what they do. Reference their product, recent blog post, job listing, or company news.

**Good examples:**

- "I came across your new product launch on Product Hunt — congrats on the traction."  
- "I read your post on scaling SaaS customer support and thought your point about response times was spot on."  
- "I noticed you recently expanded your Manchester office — exciting growth."  

**Bad examples:**

- "I hope this email finds you well." (Generic filler)  
- "I'm a freelance designer based in Bristol." (About you, not them)  
- "I'm reaching out to introduce myself." (Obvious and irrelevant)  

### Line 2: What you do (one sentence, no jargon)

State your service in plain language. Do not say "I create immersive brand experiences." Say "I design brand identities for B2B SaaS companies."

**Good examples:**

- "I help early-stage SaaS companies redesign outdated websites that do not convert."  
- "I build custom Shopify themes for independent UK retailers."  
- "I write case studies and landing page copy for fintech startups."  

**Bad examples:**

- "I'm a multidisciplinary creative who specialises in holistic brand storytelling." (Vague)  
- "I offer a full suite of design and development services." (Too broad)  
- "I'm a top-rated designer with 10 years of experience." (Bragging, no specifics)  

### Line 3: Proof or value (show, don't tell)

Give one example of past work, a relevant suggestion, or a quick win you could deliver. Make it specific to them.

**Good examples:**

- "I recently redesigned a SaaS site for a company at your stage and increased trial signups by 40%."  
- "I noticed your pricing page buries the CTA below the fold — I could suggest a few quick fixes."  
- "I helped a similar fintech client write 10 case studies in 6 weeks — happy to share examples."  

**Bad examples:**

- "I've worked with top brands like [vague claim]." (No proof)  
- "I can help you grow your business." (Too vague)  
- "Check out my portfolio." (Pushy, no context)  

### Line 4: Low-friction CTA (ask for permission, not commitment)

Do not ask for a 30-minute call. Ask for permission to share a brief, example, or suggestion.

**Good examples:**

- "Worth a quick 5-minute call to see if there is a fit?"  
- "Should I send over a couple of examples?"  
- "Happy to share a few suggestions if you are open to it?"  

**Bad examples:**

- "Let's hop on a call to discuss." (Too much friction)  
- "Can I send you my portfolio?" (Pushy)  
- "Let me know if you need help." (Passive, no action)  

### Putting it together (50–75 words max)

Subject: Quick question about your new SaaS launch

> Hi Sarah,
>
> I came across your new product launch on Product Hunt — congrats on the traction.
>
> I help early-stage SaaS companies redesign outdated websites that do not convert. I recently redesigned a site for a company at your stage and increased trial signups by 40%.
>
> Worth a quick 5-minute call to see if there is a fit?
>
> Best,  
> Tom

That is 62 words. No fluff. No fake urgency. Just research, value, and a low-friction ask.

## Subject line bank — 20 copy-paste options

Your subject line determines whether the email gets opened. Keep it under 50 characters, make it about them (not you), and avoid anything that sounds like a sales pitch.

### Research-based subject lines (best for cold email):

1. Quick question about [their product/company]  
2. [Their company] + [your service]?  
3. Thought on [specific thing they published/launched]  
4. Quick idea for [specific page/feature]  
5. [Their recent milestone] — congrats  
6. Loved your post on [specific topic]  
7. Quick suggestion for [specific thing]  
8. [Your niche] + [their niche]?  

### Problem-aware subject lines (if you know their pain point):

9. Reducing [specific problem] for [their niche]  
10. Faster [specific outcome] for [their company type]  
11. Your [specific page/feature] — quick thought  
12. Quick win for [specific challenge]  

### Curiosity-based subject lines (use sparingly):

13. Quick favour?  
14. One question for you  
15. 2-minute ask  
16. Quick follow-up  

### Do NOT use:

✗ "Collaboration opportunity"  
✗ "Following up"  
✗ "Introduction"  
✗ "Quick question" (without context)  
✗ "Can I pick your brain?"  
✗ Anything with emojis, all caps, or exclamation marks  

Stick to research-based or problem-aware subject lines. They convert best.

## Follow-up email scripts — Day 3, Day 7, break-up

Most replies come after a follow-up, not the first email. Here is the sequence.

### Follow-up 1 — Day 3 (bump with added value)

Send 3–4 days after the first email. Add value — do not just say "following up."

**Subject:** Re: [Original subject line]

> Hi [First name],
>
> Just bumping this to the top of your inbox. I realise you are probably swamped.
>
> In case it is helpful, here is a quick example of [specific thing: a site I redesigned / a case study I wrote / a dashboard I built] for [similar niche or company]: [link].
>
> Let me know if you want to chat — no pressure either way.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

---

### Follow-up 2 — Day 7 (assume no interest, offer easy out)

Send 7 days after the first follow-up (10–11 days after the original email).

**Subject:** Re: [Original subject line]

> Hi [First name],
>
> I sent a note last week about [specific thing: redesigning your site / writing case studies / building a custom feature]. I'm guessing it's not a priority right now — no worries if so.
>
> Should I close your file, or is this something you might revisit in the next few months?
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

This is called a **break-up email**. It gives the recipient an easy out and often triggers a reply because it removes pressure.

---

### Follow-up 3 — Break-up (assume they are not interested, move on)

If no reply after follow-up 2, send one final email and then stop.

**Subject:** Re: [Original subject line]

> Hi [First name],
>
> I'll assume you are not interested right now — totally fine. I'll close your file.
>
> If things change, feel free to reach out. My details are below.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]  
> [Your email]  
> [Your website]

Do not chase beyond three emails total (original + 2 follow-ups). If they have not replied by then, they are not interested. Move on.

## UK PECR and GDPR — what you need to know (high-level, not legal advice)

Cold email in the UK is governed by **PECR (Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations)** and **GDPR**. This is not legal advice — consult a solicitor for compliance guidance. But here is what most UK freelancers need to know.

### B2B cold email is generally permitted when:

✓ You are emailing a **business email address** (e.g. firstname@company.co.uk, not a personal Gmail)  
✓ You include your **real name and business details** (no fake identities or misleading sender names)  
✓ You provide an **unsubscribe option** (e.g. "Reply UNSUBSCRIBE if you do not want to hear from me")  
✓ You respect unsubscribe requests **immediately** (within 24–48 hours max)  
✓ You do not **mass-blast** the same email to hundreds of people without personalisation  

### B2C cold email (emailing individual consumers) has stricter rules:

✓ You need **prior consent** (opt-in) to email personal addresses like Gmail, Hotmail, etc.  
✓ This means cold email to **individual consumers is generally NOT permitted** unless they opted in  
✓ Emailing **sole traders at their business address** is a grey area — seek legal advice  

### What to include in every cold email (UK compliance basics):

✓ Your **real name**  
✓ Your **business name** (if you trade under one)  
✓ Your **contact details** (email + phone or website)  
✓ An **unsubscribe line** (e.g. "Reply STOP to unsubscribe")  

**Example footer:**

> [Your name]  
> [Your business name]  
> [Your email]  
> [Your website]  
>
> To unsubscribe, reply STOP.

### What NOT to do:

✗ Send emails from a fake name or misleading sender address  
✗ Hide your business identity or contact details  
✗ Ignore unsubscribe requests  
✗ Buy email lists and blast them without research  
✗ Send cold emails to personal Gmail addresses of individual consumers  

For official guidance, see the [ICO (Information Commissioner's Office) website](https://ico.org.uk). When in doubt, consult a solicitor who specialises in PECR and GDPR compliance.

## Common cold email mistakes (and how to fix them)

### Mistake 1: Sending the same email to 100 people

**The problem:** Copy-pasting the same template to everyone with no personalisation. Recipients can tell. It feels like spam.

**The fix:** Research each recipient. Reference something specific about their company, product, or content. Spend 5 minutes per email. Send 10 researched emails, not 100 generic blasts.

---

### Mistake 2: Making it all about you

**The problem:** Opening with "I'm a freelance designer with 10 years of experience..." The client does not care about you yet. They care about their problem.

**The fix:** Open with one line about them. Prove you researched. Then state what you do in one sentence.

---

### Mistake 3: Asking for a 30-minute call in the first email

**The problem:** Too much friction. A 30-minute call is a big ask for someone who has never heard of you.

**The fix:** Ask for a 5-minute call, or offer to send examples first. Lower the friction.

---

### Mistake 4: No follow-up (or too many follow-ups)

**The problem:** Either you send one email and give up, or you chase weekly for a month. Both fail.

**The fix:** Follow up twice. Once at day 3, once at day 7. If no reply, send a break-up email and move on.

---

### Mistake 5: No proof or specifics

**The problem:** "I can help you grow your business." Too vague. No evidence.

**The fix:** Share one specific example: "I redesigned a SaaS site for a company at your stage and increased trial signups by 40%."

---

### Mistake 6: Attachments in the first email

**The problem:** Attachments look like spam. They also trigger spam filters.

**The fix:** No attachments in the first email. Link to your portfolio or offer to send examples in the follow-up.

---

### Mistake 7: Fake urgency or scarcity

**The problem:** "I only have 2 slots left this month!" If you are cold-emailing, you probably have slots. Do not lie.

**The fix:** Skip urgency. Just ask if they are open to a conversation.
