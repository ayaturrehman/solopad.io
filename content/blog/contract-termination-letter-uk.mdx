## How contract termination works for UK freelancers — convenience vs cause

Before you send a termination letter, understand the two types of termination and what your contract allows.

### Termination for convenience (with notice)

**"For convenience"** means either party can end the contract without giving a reason, as long as they give the agreed notice period (e.g. 14 days, 30 days).

**Example contract clause:**

> Either party may terminate this contract by giving the other party 30 days' written notice.

If your contract has this clause, you (or the client) can terminate at any time by giving 30 days' notice. No explanation required. The contract simply runs for another 30 days, then ends.

**What happens during the notice period:**

✓ Work continues as normal (unless you agree to stop earlier)  
✓ You invoice for work done during the notice period  
✓ The client pays as usual  
✓ On the last day, you hand over any work in progress (if paid) and close the project  

**When to use it:**

- You want to end a retainer or rolling contract  
- The project is not working out but neither side has breached the contract  
- You are too busy to continue or need to focus on other clients  
- The client wants to bring the work in-house or go with another supplier  

### Termination for cause (breach, non-payment, or material failure)

**"For cause"** means you (or the client) are ending the contract because the other side has breached it in a serious way.

**Example contract clause:**

> Either party may terminate this contract immediately by written notice if the other party:  
> (a) Fails to pay any invoice by the due date and the amount remains unpaid for 14 days after a written payment reminder  
> (b) Breaches any material term of the contract and fails to remedy the breach within 7 days of written notice  

**Common causes for a freelancer to terminate:**

✓ Client has not paid two or more invoices (repeated non-payment)  
✓ Client repeatedly changes the brief or scope without agreeing to a change order (scope creep that breaches the agreed deliverables)  
✓ Client fails to provide materials, feedback, or approvals, preventing you from completing the work (breach of client responsibilities)  
✓ Client uses your work before paying for it (breach of IP terms)  

**What happens when you terminate for cause:**

✓ The contract ends immediately (no notice period required)  
✓ You invoice for all work completed to the termination date  
✓ You keep any deposits and charge any kill fee or cancellation compensation stated in the contract  
✓ You do NOT hand over work, files, or IP until the final invoice is paid  
✓ You may add statutory interest and late payment compensation if the termination was caused by non-payment  

**When to use it:**

- The client has breached the contract in a serious way  
- They have not paid and you have already sent reminders  
- They have refused to correct a breach after you gave them a cure period (e.g. 7 days)  
- Continuing the contract would harm you financially or reputationally  

**Tip:** Termination for cause is stronger than termination for convenience. It shows the client breached first, which protects you if they later claim you walked away without justification. Always cite the specific breach and clause number when you terminate for cause.

### What if the contract does not say how to terminate?

If your contract is silent on termination (no termination clause, no notice period), you can still terminate by giving **reasonable notice** in writing.

**Reasonable notice** depends on:

- The length of the contract (a one-month project might need 7 days' notice; a 12-month retainer might need 30 days)  
- The nature of the work (mission-critical systems need longer handover periods than one-off design projects)  
- Industry norms (most UK freelance sectors treat 14-30 days as reasonable for rolling contracts)  

**Example:**

You are three months into a six-month website project. The client goes silent for four weeks and stops replying to emails. The contract has no termination clause.

You email:

> "I am writing to give you formal notice that I am terminating our contract for the [Project Name] website with effect from [14 or 30 days from now]. I have made repeated attempts to contact you regarding approvals and feedback, without response. Reasonable notice for a project of this length is 14 days. I will invoice for work completed to [termination date]."

**Source:** English contract law recognises an implied right to terminate on reasonable notice when the contract is silent. See [Practical Law — Termination for convenience](https://uk.practicallaw.thomsonreuters.com/) (subscription required, but the principle is that indefinite contracts can be ended on reasonable notice).

## The final invoice — what to charge when a contract ends

When a contract terminates, you invoice for work done, any amounts the contract entitles you to (deposits, expenses, kill fees, notice-period payments), and any overdue invoices. Here is how to calculate it.

### 1. Work completed to the termination date

Invoice for all work you delivered before the termination date. This includes:

✓ Completed deliverables or milestones  
✓ Work in progress (if measurable — e.g. 60% of a design round completed, or 12 hours worked on a task)  
✓ Time spent on revisions, calls, emails, and project management (if you charge for these)  

**How to calculate:**

- **Fixed-price projects:** Invoice based on milestones or percentage of completion (e.g. if the project was £4,000 and you delivered 3 out of 5 milestones, invoice £2,400)  
- **Hourly or day-rate projects:** Invoice for hours or days worked, at your agreed rate  
- **Retainers:** Invoice for the days or hours worked in the current retainer period (see worked example below)  

### 2. Deposits and advance payments

**If you already received a deposit:**

- Deduct the deposit from the final invoice total  
- If the deposit exceeds the work done, refund the difference (unless your contract says deposits are non-refundable)  
- If the work done exceeds the deposit, invoice the balance  

**Example:**

- Deposit paid: £1,200  
- Work done: £1,800  
- Final invoice: £1,800 - £1,200 = **£600**  

**If your contract says deposits are non-refundable:**

You keep the deposit regardless of how much work you did, AND invoice for any further amounts due. Check your contract's deposit clause.

### 3. Expenses

Invoice for expenses you incurred and are entitled to recover under the contract:

✓ Stock images, fonts, plugins purchased for the project  
✓ Third-party services (e.g. hosting, domain registration)  
✓ Travel costs (if the contract allows)  
✓ Materials or equipment purchased for the project  

**Tip:** Only invoice expenses that the contract allows and that you agreed in writing. Attach receipts if the client requests proof.

### 4. Notice-period payments

If the client terminated without giving the required notice (e.g. your contract says 30 days' notice and they gave 10 days), you may be entitled to compensation for the shortfall.

**Example:**

- Your contract requires 30 days' notice  
- Client gives 10 days' notice  
- You are entitled to payment for the remaining 20 days (at your retainer rate or pro-rata rate)  

**How to calculate (retainer example):**

- Monthly retainer: £2,400  
- Daily rate: £2,400 ÷ 20 working days = £120/day  
- Shortfall: 20 days × £120 = £2,400  

**Invoice wording:**

> Notice period shortfall: 20 days at £120/day (contract required 30 days' notice, 10 days given) = £2,400

**Tip:** If the client disputes this, cite the termination clause and state that the notice period is a contractual obligation.

### 5. Cancellation fees or kill fees (if in the contract)

If your contract includes a kill fee or cancellation compensation clause, and the client is terminating early, invoice the kill fee as stated in the contract.

**Example:**

- Total project fee: £5,000  
- Kill fee clause: 50% of project fee if cancelled after first deliverable  
- Client cancels after you delivered wireframes and first design round  
- Kill fee: £5,000 × 50% = **£2,500**  

**Less any payments already made:**

- Deposit paid: £1,500  
- Kill fee invoice: £2,500 - £1,500 = **£1,000**  

For full guidance on kill fees, read our [kill fee clause template guide](/blog/kill-fee-clause-template-uk).

### 6. Statutory interest and late payment compensation

If the client terminated the contract and still owes you money on earlier invoices, you can add statutory interest and fixed compensation under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998.

**Statutory interest:** 8% per annum + Bank of England base rate (currently 4.75%, so total 12.75%), calculated daily from the invoice due date  
**Fixed compensation:**

- Invoices up to £999.99: £40  
- Invoices £1,000 to £9,999.99: £70  
- Invoices £10,000 or more: £100  

**Example:**

- Invoice £1,200, due 1 October, paid 40 days late on 10 November  
- Statutory interest: £1,200 × 12.75% ÷ 365 × 40 = £16.77  
- Fixed compensation: £70  
- Late payment recovery: £16.77 + £70 = **£86.77**  

For full guidance and a calculator, read our [statutory interest guide](/blog/statutory-interest-uk-freelancers).

## Worked example — £2,400/month retainer ending mid-month

Here is a step-by-step final invoice calculation for a UK freelancer on a monthly retainer that ends mid-month.

**Scenario:**

**Freelancer:** Jordan at Oak & Pixel (designer, sole trader, not VAT registered)  
**Client:** Summit Media Ltd (marketing agency)  
**Retainer:** £2,400/month (20 working days per month, equivalent to £120/day), rolling monthly contract with 30 days' notice  
**Start date:** 1 September 2026  
**Termination notice received:** 8 October 2026 (client terminates by email, gives 7 days' notice)  
**Termination date stated by client:** 15 October 2026  

**The problem:**

The contract requires 30 days' notice. Client only gave 7 days. Jordan needs to calculate the final invoice, including the notice-period shortfall.

---

### Step 1: Check the termination clause

Jordan's contract (clause 9) says:

> Either party may terminate this contract by giving the other party 30 days' written notice. The notice period begins on the date written notice is received.

Client sent notice on 8 October, so 30 days from then is 7 November 2026. But client said they want to end on 15 October (only 7 days).

Jordan's options:

1. Insist on working the full 30-day notice period (ending 7 November)  
2. Agree to end early (15 October) but invoice for the shortfall  

Jordan replies within 24 hours and chooses option 2 (end early but invoice the shortfall).

---

### Step 2: Calculate work done to termination date (15 October)

Jordan worked:

- September: Full month (£2,400 already invoiced and paid)  
- October: 1 Oct to 15 Oct = 11 working days (including the 15th)  

**October pro-rata fee:**

- Monthly retainer: £2,400  
- Daily rate: £2,400 ÷ 20 working days = £120/day  
- Days worked: 11 days  
- October fee: 11 × £120 = **£1,320**

---

### Step 3: Calculate notice-period shortfall

Contract required 30 days' notice. Client gave 7 days. Shortfall: 30 - 7 = 23 days.

**Notice shortfall payment:**

- 23 days × £120/day = **£2,760**

Jordan explains in the termination reply email:

> "Your notice dated 8 October gives 7 days' notice (to 15 October). Our contract (clause 9) requires 30 days' notice. I am willing to end the contract on 15 October as you requested, but under the contract you are required to pay for the 23-day shortfall in the notice period. This is calculated as 23 working days at £120/day = £2,760."

---

### Step 4: Total final invoice

| Description | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Work done 1-15 October (11 days at £120/day) | £1,320.00 |
| Notice period shortfall (23 days at £120/day) | £2,760.00 |
| **Total due** | **£4,080.00** |

**OR, if the client disputes the notice shortfall:**

Jordan can choose to invoice only for work done (£1,320) and waive the shortfall to avoid a dispute — but this means accepting less than the contract entitles them to. Most freelancers invoice the full amount and negotiate down only if the client pushes back.

---

### Step 5: Handover

Jordan states in the termination reply:

> "Upon receipt of full payment (£4,080), I will provide:  
> ✓ All design files created during the retainer (AI, Figma, exported PNGs)  
> ✓ Access to the Figma workspace (I will transfer ownership to your account)  
> ✓ Handover document listing all active projects and their status  
>
> Intellectual property rights transfer on full payment as per clause 7 of our contract."

---

### Step 6: Outcome

Summit Media Ltd's accounts team queries the £2,760 notice shortfall. Jordan sends a polite reply:

> "The notice shortfall is calculated under clause 9 of our contract, which requires 30 days' notice. You requested an early end date (15 October instead of 7 November), so the shortfall is the difference: 23 working days at the daily retainer rate of £120. This is standard contract law — if you end a contract early without the required notice, you pay compensation for the shortfall. I am happy to discuss if you would prefer to extend the retainer to 7 November and pay the standard monthly fee instead."

Summit Media agrees to pay the full £4,080. Payment clears on 28 October. Jordan sends the files and closes the project.

## Handover rules — files, logins, and IP transfer

When a contract ends, the client usually expects you to hand over:

✓ All work files (design files, code, content, graphics)  
✓ Access credentials (logins, API keys, admin accounts)  
✓ Intellectual property rights (ownership of the work you created)  

But **when** you hand these over, and **whether** you must hand them over at all, depends on your contract.

### IP transfers on full payment (standard)

Most UK freelance contracts say:

> Intellectual property rights in the Deliverables transfer to the Client upon receipt of full payment.

This means:

✓ Until the client pays the final invoice in full, YOU own the IP  
✓ You are NOT obliged to hand over files, source code, or work until payment clears  
✓ If they want the files before payment, they must pay upfront or agree to immediate cleared funds (bank transfer, not cheque)  

**What to say in your termination letter:**

> "Under clause [X] of our contract, intellectual property rights transfer on full payment. I will provide all work files and transfer IP rights upon receipt of cleared funds for the final invoice (£[Amount]). Payment is due within 14 days. If you require the files sooner, I can arrange immediate handover upon receipt of an instant bank transfer."

**Tip:** Do not hand over files before payment "as a gesture of goodwill" unless you trust the client completely. Once they have the files, your leverage to get paid drops to zero.

### What if the contract says IP transfers on delivery (not payment)?

Some contracts (especially client-drafted contracts) say:

> Intellectual property rights transfer to the Client upon delivery of the Deliverables.

If your contract says this, you must hand over the files on delivery, even if they have not paid yet.

**What to do:**

✓ Check your contract's IP clause before you assume you can withhold files  
✓ If IP transfers on delivery, hand over the files but chase payment aggressively using statutory interest demands and debt recovery  
✓ Next time, negotiate the IP clause BEFORE you sign (always push for "IP transfers on full payment")  

For more on IP clauses, read our [freelance contract template guide](/blog/freelance-contract-template-guide).

### Logins and access credentials

**If you created or manage logins on behalf of the client** (e.g. WordPress admin, hosting account, social media accounts), your obligations depend on the contract:

✓ If the contract says you must hand over logins, do so once you are paid  
✓ If the contract is silent, reasonable practice is to hand over logins once the final invoice is paid  
✓ Do NOT delete accounts, change passwords, or lock the client out as leverage for payment — this could be seen as holding their property hostage and may breach the contract  

**Best practice:**

State in your termination letter:

> "Upon full payment, I will provide a handover document listing all access credentials, including [WordPress admin, hosting, domain registrar, email accounts]. I will transfer ownership where applicable (e.g. transfer domain registration to your account). I will not retain any access after handover."

**Security note:** After handover, advise the client to change all passwords immediately. You should delete any stored credentials from your password manager.

### Work in progress — what if the work is unfinished?

If the contract ends mid-project and the work is incomplete:

✓ You are NOT required to finish it (unless the contract or termination agreement says so)  
✓ Invoice for work done to date (as percentage of completion, hours worked, or milestones delivered)  
✓ Hand over work in progress files only if the contract requires it AND only when paid  
✓ Mark the files clearly: "Work in progress — not final — supplied as-is"  

**What to say:**

> "The project was terminated before completion. I will provide work in progress files (wireframes, first design round, 60% of homepage build) upon full payment. These files are supplied 'as-is' and are not final or approved deliverables. You will need to complete or adapt the work yourself or engage another supplier."

## Consumer cancellation rights — the 14-day distance-selling rule

If you provide services to an **individual consumer** (a person buying for personal use, not for their business), and the contract was made **online, by phone, or away from your business premises** (distance or off-premises contract), the consumer has special cancellation rights under UK law.

### The 14-day cancellation right (Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013)

**The rule:**

The consumer has **14 days from the contract date** to cancel, for any reason, without penalty — **unless** they expressly requested you start work immediately and you complete the work within the 14-day period.

**What this means for freelancers:**

✓ If you quote a consumer online (via email, website, or DM) and they accept, they can cancel within 14 days  
✓ You must tell them about the cancellation right BEFORE the contract is formed (in your quote, terms, or proposal)  
✓ If they cancel within 14 days, you must refund any payment, **except** for work already completed (you can charge pro-rata for work done if they asked you to start immediately)  

**How to protect yourself:**

✓ When quoting a consumer, include this wording in your terms:

> **Cancellation right for consumers:**  
> You have the right to cancel this contract within 14 days of the date you accept our quote, for any reason, and receive a full refund of any payment made.  
> If you want us to start work within the 14-day cancellation period, you must make an express request in writing. By requesting that we start work immediately, you acknowledge that if we complete the work during the cancellation period, you will lose your right to cancel and will be liable for the full fee.

✓ If the consumer asks you to start work immediately, get their written agreement (email is fine):

> "I understand that I am requesting [Freelancer Name] to start work immediately, before the 14-day cancellation period ends. I agree that if the work is completed before the 14-day period ends, I will lose my right to cancel and will pay the full fee."

**Source:**

- [Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/contents/made) — UK legislation  
- [Citizens Advice — Cancelling a service you've arranged](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/changed-your-mind/cancelling-a-service-youve-arranged/) — Consumer guidance  
- [Which? — Your consumer rights when buying services](https://www.which.co.uk/consumer-rights/regulation/consumer-contracts-regulations-aCr8O2g0YuEy) — Plain-English explainer  

**B2B clients (business to business) do NOT have this right.** The 14-day cancellation right applies only to consumers.

**Tip:** If you are unsure whether a client is a consumer or a business, ask them at the quote stage: "Are you purchasing these services for business use or personal use?" If personal, treat them as a consumer.

## Termination decision checklist — before you send the letter

Use this checklist before you send a termination letter to make sure you have covered everything.

### Before you decide to terminate:

✓ **Read the termination clause in your contract** — Does it allow termination for convenience? What notice period is required? Does it allow termination for cause?  
✓ **Identify the reason** — Are you terminating for convenience (no breach), or for cause (breach, non-payment, scope creep)?  
✓ **Check if there is a cure period** — Does the contract require you to give the client a chance to fix the breach before you terminate? (E.g. "7 days to remedy the breach")  
✓ **Calculate what they owe you** — Work done, deposits, expenses, kill fees, notice-period payments, overdue invoices  
✓ **Gather evidence** — If terminating for cause, collect emails, invoices, reminders, and any proof of the breach  

### Before you send the termination letter:

✓ **Use the correct method** — Does the contract require registered post, or is email acceptable? If the contract is silent, email is standard.  
✓ **Address it to the right person** — Send to the client's main business contact, or (for companies) the registered office address if required  
✓ **State the termination date clearly** — Count forward from today by the notice period, or (for cause) state "with immediate effect"  
✓ **Cite the contract clause** — Reference the termination clause number (e.g. "in accordance with clause 9")  
✓ **Explain the reason (if terminating for cause)** — State the breach clearly and cite the clause they breached  
✓ **State what happens next** — Final invoice, handover, IP transfer  
✓ **Keep a copy** — Save the termination letter and proof of sending (email sent confirmation, or registered post receipt)  

### After you send the termination letter:

✓ **Stop work immediately (if terminating for cause)** — Do not provide any further services  
✓ **Send the final invoice promptly** — Within 3-7 days of the termination date  
✓ **Do not hand over files or IP until paid** — Unless the contract requires it or they pay upfront  
✓ **Chase payment if they do not pay** — Use statutory interest demands, letter before action, and debt recovery

## FAQ

### Can I terminate a freelance contract by email?

Yes, if your contract allows email notices or is silent on the method. Email is standard for UK freelancers.

**How to do it:**

✓ Send to the client's main business email address (the one they use for project comms)  
✓ Use a clear subject line: "Notice of contract termination — [Your Name] — [Client Project Name]"  
✓ State the termination date clearly in the email body  
✓ Keep a copy of the sent email (screenshot or save to PDF)  

**If the contract requires registered post:**

Follow the contract exactly. Send by Royal Mail Signed For or Special Delivery. Keep the proof of posting receipt. You can also send a courtesy copy by email, but the registered post is the formal notice.

**Tip:** If you want extra proof, send the email AND send a printed copy by registered post. State in the email: "This notice is also being sent by registered post to [Address]."

### Do I need to give a reason when I terminate a contract?

**For convenience (with notice): No.**

If you are terminating with the agreed notice period, you do not need to give a reason. The contract allows either party to terminate by giving notice — no explanation required.

**You can be polite:**

> "I am writing to give notice of termination as permitted under clause 9 of our contract. I have enjoyed working on [Project Name], but I need to focus on other commitments."

But you do not have to justify your decision.

**For cause (breach, non-payment): Yes.**

If you are terminating for cause, you MUST state the reason and cite the breach. This protects your right to terminate without notice (or with shorter notice) and supports your claim if they dispute it.

**Example:**

> "I am terminating this contract with immediate effect due to non-payment. Invoice INV-2026-089 in the amount of £1,200 was due on 1 October 2026 and remains unpaid 30 days later, despite written reminders sent on 8 October and 15 October. This is a material breach of clause 5 (Payment Terms)."

### What if my contract does not say how to terminate?

You can still terminate by giving **reasonable notice** in writing.

**Reasonable notice** depends on:

✓ The length and nature of the contract (one-week project = 2-3 days; six-month retainer = 14-30 days)  
✓ Industry norms (14-30 days is standard for rolling contracts)  
✓ The client's ability to find a replacement (mission-critical work needs longer handover)  

**What to say:**

> "I am writing to give you notice that I am terminating our contract for [Project Name] with effect from [Date — 14 or 30 days from now]. Although our contract does not specify a notice period, I am providing [14 / 30] days' notice, which I believe is reasonable for a contract of this length and nature."

**Source:** English contract law recognises an implied right to terminate on reasonable notice when the contract is indefinite or silent on termination.

### Can the client refuse to pay for work done if I terminate the contract?

No. Even if you terminate the contract, the client owes you for work completed to the termination date.

**Why:**

✓ You performed the work in good faith under the contract  
✓ The client received value from that work  
✓ Termination does not cancel debts already incurred  

**What to do if they refuse to pay:**

1. Invoice promptly with a clear breakdown (hours, days, or milestones delivered)  
2. If they do not pay, send a polite payment reminder 7 days after the due date  
3. If still unpaid, send a statutory interest demand (see our [statutory interest guide](/blog/statutory-interest-uk-freelancers))  
4. If still unpaid, send a letter before action (see our [letter before action template](/blog/letter-before-action-template-uk))  
5. If they ignore it, file a claim via [Money Claim Online](https://www.gov.uk/make-court-claim-for-money)  

**Tip:** If they claim the work is unusable or substandard, ask them to specify the defects in writing. If they approved the work at the time, they cannot later claim it was defective. Save all approval emails and feedback.

### What happens to deposits and advance payments when a contract is terminated?

It depends on your contract and who terminated.

**If the client terminates:**

✓ You keep the deposit (if your contract says deposits are non-refundable)  
✓ OR you refund the unused portion after deducting work done (if the contract does not say deposits are non-refundable)  

**If you terminate for convenience:**

✓ You may need to refund the unused deposit amount after deducting work done (unless the contract says otherwise)  

**If you terminate for cause (their breach):**

✓ You keep the full deposit AND invoice for further amounts due (work done, kill fee, notice shortfall)  

**Example:**

- Deposit: £1,200  
- Work done when contract terminates: £900  
- Your contract says deposits are non-refundable for work done  
- Client terminates: You keep £900, refund £300  
- You terminate for cause: You keep £1,200 (their breach = they forfeit the deposit)  

**Check your contract's deposit clause carefully.** If it says "non-refundable", you keep it. If it is silent, you may need to refund unused amounts.

### Do I have to hand over files and passwords when I terminate?

**Files (work product, deliverables):**

✓ Only if the contract requires it AND only when you have been paid in full (unless the contract says IP transfers on delivery, not payment)  
✓ Your contract's IP clause usually says: "Intellectual property transfers on full payment"  
✓ Until paid, YOU own the files — you are not obliged to hand them over  

**Access credentials (logins, passwords, admin accounts):**

✓ Hand them over once the final invoice is paid, unless the contract says otherwise  
✓ Do NOT lock the client out or delete accounts as leverage — this could breach the contract  
✓ After handover, advise the client to change all passwords  

**Best practice:**

State clearly in your termination letter:

> "Upon receipt of full payment of the final invoice (£[Amount]), I will provide all work files and access credentials. Intellectual property rights transfer on payment as per clause [X] of our contract."

### Can a consumer client cancel within 14 days under the Consumer Contracts Regulations?

Yes, if:

✓ The client is an **individual consumer** (buying for personal use, not business)  
✓ The contract was made **online, by phone, or away from your business premises** (distance or off-premises contract)  
✓ The consumer did NOT expressly request you start work immediately AND you did not complete the work within the 14-day period  

**What happens if they cancel within 14 days:**

✓ You must refund any payment made  
✓ EXCEPT you can charge pro-rata for work already completed (if they asked you to start immediately)  

**Business clients (B2B) do NOT have this right.** The 14-day cancellation right applies only to consumers.

**Source:** [Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/contents/made)

For more on consumer vs B2B rules, read our [terms and conditions template guide](/blog/terms-and-conditions-template-uk).

### What if the client terminates but still owes money on an earlier invoice?

Their termination does not cancel existing debts.

**What to do:**

✓ State in your termination reply: "Termination of the contract does not cancel your obligation to pay outstanding invoices. Invoice [Number] for £[Amount] was due on [Date] and remains unpaid."  
✓ Add statutory interest and fixed compensation under the Late Payment Act  
✓ Chase the overdue invoice separately from the final termination invoice  
✓ If they refuse to pay, send a letter before action and file a claim in small claims court  

**Tip:** If the client is disputing the earlier invoice, deal with that dispute separately. Do not let them use the termination as an excuse to avoid paying debts they already owe.
