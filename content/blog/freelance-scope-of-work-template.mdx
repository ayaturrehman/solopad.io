Your client just asked if you can "quickly add" three extra pages to the website project. No mention of extra budget. No acknowledgement that this was never in the brief.

You pause. You want to be helpful. But you also know this is how unpaid overtime starts.

This is scope creep. And it happens because most freelancers never define what is included and what is not before the work starts.

The fix: a clear scope of work (SOW) that spells out deliverables, exclusions, revision limits, and how change requests are handled.

This guide gives you a pasteable UK freelance scope of work template, explains when to use it, shows you how to pair it with your contract, and includes copy-paste scripts for handling change orders without damaging the client relationship.

## Why you need a scope of work (and how it stops scope creep)

Scope creep is the silent cashflow killer.

A client hires you for a logo and brand guidelines. Halfway through, they ask for Instagram templates. Then a business card design. Then a pitch deck.

None of that was in the original brief. But because you never wrote down what was included and what was not, the client assumes it is all part of the package.

You end up working twice as long for the same fee.

A scope of work prevents this. It is a document that defines:

✓ Exactly what you will deliver  
✓ What is explicitly excluded  
✓ How many revision rounds are included  
✓ What happens when the client asks for changes outside the agreed scope  
✓ Timeline, milestones, and payment schedule  

Think of it as a shared map. Both you and the client know where the boundaries are. When the client asks for something outside those boundaries, you can point to the SOW and say: "That is outside scope. I can add it as a change order. Would you like a quote?"

No awkwardness. No resentment. Just clear expectations from the start.

## Scope of work vs contract — what is the difference?

A **contract** is the legal framework. It covers payment terms, liability, intellectual property, confidentiality, termination, and dispute resolution. It protects both parties if something goes wrong.

A **scope of work** is the delivery plan. It defines what you are building, how long it will take, what is included, and how changes are managed.

You need both.

Many freelancers combine them: the contract is the main document, and the scope of work is attached as **Appendix A** or **Schedule 1**. That works well.

You can also send the scope of work first — before the contract — to get client approval on deliverables and budget. Once the scope is agreed, you send the contract to formalise the legal terms.

Either way, both documents should be signed or confirmed in writing before you start work.

## What to include in your scope of work — section by section

Here is the structure most UK freelancers use, with pasteable examples for each section.

### 1. Project overview

A one-paragraph summary of the project. This sets context and confirms both sides are aligned on the goal.

**Example:**

> **Project:** Brand identity design for Northline Consulting  
> **Objective:** Create a professional, modern brand identity to support the launch of Northline's strategic advisory services. Deliverables include logo, colour palette, typography system, and brand guidelines.  
> **Start date:** 10 September 2026  
> **Estimated completion:** 10 October 2026  

### 2. Deliverables (with definition of done)

This is the most important section. List every deliverable, and define what "done" means for each one.

Vague deliverables invite scope creep. "A logo" could mean one concept or ten. "Brand guidelines" could be 5 pages or 50.

Be specific.

**Example:**

> **Deliverables:**  
>  
> ✓ **Logo design**  
> — Three initial logo concepts (black and white)  
> — One chosen concept refined in full colour  
> — Final logo supplied in the following formats: AI, EPS, SVG, PNG (transparent), JPG  
>  
> ✓ **Colour palette**  
> — Primary and secondary colour palette (up to 6 colours)  
> — Hex, RGB, CMYK values provided  
>  
> ✓ **Typography system**  
> — Recommendation of two typefaces (one for headings, one for body text)  
> — Licensing guidance and links to font sources  
>  
> ✓ **Brand guidelines document**  
> — PDF document (10–12 pages) covering logo usage, colour, typography, tone of voice, and applications  
> — Supplied as editable Figma file and PDF export  

Notice the detail. The client now knows exactly what they are getting. And you know exactly what you need to deliver.

### 3. Out of scope (what is not included)

This is where you prevent surprise requests.

List common extras that clients often assume are included. By naming them explicitly as out of scope, you make it clear they require a separate quote.

**Example:**

> **Out of scope (not included in this project):**  
>  
> ✗ Website design or development  
> ✗ Social media templates or content creation  
> ✗ Business card, letterhead, or stationery design  
> ✗ Animation or motion graphics  
> ✗ Printed materials or print production  
> ✗ Trademark registration or legal advice  
>  
> If any of the above are required, they can be added as a separate project or change order. A quote will be provided upon request.

This section does two things:

1. It manages client expectations upfront.
2. It gives you a reference point when the client asks for extras later.

### 4. Timeline and milestones

Break the project into phases. For each phase, state what you will deliver and when.

**Example:**

> **Timeline:**  
>  
> **Week 1 (10–16 September):** Discovery and moodboards  
> — Client kickoff meeting (1 hour)  
> — Moodboard and visual direction (3 options)  
> — Client feedback due by 16 September  
>  
> **Week 2 (17–23 September):** Initial logo concepts  
> — Three logo concepts presented  
> — Client selects one concept for refinement  
> — Feedback due by 23 September  
>  
> **Week 3 (24–30 September):** Refinement and colour development  
> — Chosen logo refined in full colour  
> — Colour palette and typography recommendations  
> — Client feedback due by 30 September  
>  
> **Week 4 (1–10 October):** Brand guidelines and final delivery  
> — Brand guidelines document completed  
> — Final files delivered  
> — Project sign-off  

**Note:** Timelines depend on the client providing feedback by the stated deadlines. Delays in feedback may extend the delivery date.

### 5. Revision rounds

Define how many rounds of revisions are included, and what counts as a revision.

**Example:**

> **Revisions:**  
>  
> This project includes **two rounds of revisions** for each major deliverable (logo concepts, colour palette, brand guidelines).  
>  
> A revision is defined as changes within the agreed direction — adjustments to spacing, colour, sizing, or layout. Revisions do not include new concepts, major redesigns, or additional deliverables.  
>  
> Additional revision rounds beyond the two included are charged at £75 per hour.

This protects you from endless tweaks. Two revision rounds is standard. Three is generous. Beyond that, charge for your time.

### 6. Client responsibilities and assumptions

State what the client needs to provide for the project to stay on track.

**Example:**

> **Client responsibilities:**  
>  
> ✓ Provide feedback by the deadlines stated in the timeline  
> ✓ Supply any required brand assets, competitor examples, or reference materials by 10 September  
> ✓ Attend scheduled review meetings or provide written feedback within 48 hours  
> ✓ Respond to clarification questions within 2 business days  
>  
> **Assumptions:**  
>  
> This scope assumes:  
> — The client has final decision-making authority or can obtain internal approvals within the feedback windows  
> — No more than two stakeholders will provide feedback (to avoid conflicting directions)  
> — All content and copy for the brand guidelines will be provided by the client or written by the freelancer as part of the deliverable  

This section sets boundaries around responsiveness. If the client goes silent for two weeks, you are not obligated to hold the timeline.

### 7. Change-order process

Explain what happens when the client asks for work outside the agreed scope.

**Example:**

> **Change orders and scope changes:**  
>  
> If you request work outside the agreed scope (for example, additional logo concepts, business card design, or website mockups), I will provide a change-order quote detailing the extra cost and revised timeline.  
>  
> Change orders are invoiced separately and must be approved in writing before work begins.  
>  
> Small clarifications and minor tweaks within the agreed direction are not considered change orders.

This normalises the idea that extra work costs extra money. It is not awkward. It is professional.

### 8. Total fee and payment schedule

State the total project fee, how it will be invoiced, and the payment terms.

**Example:**

> **Total project fee:** £4,800  
>  
> **Payment schedule:**  
> — **Deposit (40%):** £1,920 — due upon signing this scope of work. Work will not commence until the deposit is received.  
> — **Mid-point (30%):** £1,440 — invoiced upon delivery of logo concepts (end of Week 2). Due within 14 days (Net 14).  
> — **Final payment (30%):** £1,440 — invoiced upon delivery of final files and brand guidelines. Due within 14 days (Net 14).  
>  
> All invoices are payable by bank transfer. Payment terms are Net 14 unless otherwise agreed.

For more on setting payment terms and managing cashflow, see our guide on [invoice payment terms for UK freelancers](/blog/invoice-payment-terms-uk).

### 9. Acceptance and sign-off

Define what "done" looks like and how the project is closed.

**Example:**

> **Acceptance criteria:**  
>  
> The project is considered complete when:  
> — All deliverables listed in Section 2 have been supplied  
> — The client has confirmed acceptance in writing (email is fine)  
> — Final payment has been received  
>  
> Once the project is signed off, the source files and final deliverables are transferred to the client. Any requests for changes after sign-off are treated as new work and charged separately.

This prevents clients from reopening the project three months later and asking for tweaks.

## Real example — brand identity project with clear scope boundaries

Let's walk through a worked example: a brand identity project for a consulting firm.

**Project:** Brand identity for Northline Consulting  
**Freelancer:** Oak & Pixel (design studio, fictional example)  
**Total fee:** £4,800  

### What is included

✓ Three initial logo concepts (black and white)  
✓ One chosen concept refined in full colour  
✓ Colour palette (up to 6 colours, with hex/RGB/CMYK values)  
✓ Typography system (two typefaces recommended)  
✓ Brand guidelines PDF (10–12 pages)  
✓ Final logo files (AI, EPS, SVG, PNG, JPG)  
✓ Two revision rounds per deliverable  

### What is not included

✗ Business card or stationery design  
✗ Website design  
✗ Social media templates  
✗ Animation or motion graphics  
✗ More than three initial logo concepts  

### Timeline

4 weeks (10 September – 10 October 2026)

### Payment schedule

£1,920 deposit (40%) upfront  
£1,440 (30%) mid-point  
£1,440 (30%) on delivery  

### What happens if the client asks for extras

Two weeks into the project, the client emails:

> "Can you also design business cards and a letterhead? We need them for a pitch next week."

This is outside scope. Here is how the freelancer responds:

> "Thanks for thinking of me for the business cards and letterhead. Those items are outside the original scope of work, which focused on logo and brand guidelines only.
>
> I can absolutely add them as a change order. Business cards and letterhead would be an additional £600 (design and print-ready files), and I can turn them around in 3 business days.
>
> Let me know if you would like me to send a formal change-order quote, and I will get started once you approve it."

The client agrees. The freelancer sends a one-page change-order document (pasteable template below) and invoices £600 separately.

Result: the client gets what they need, the freelancer gets paid for the extra work, and there is no resentment.

## Copy-paste change-order email script

When a client asks for work outside the agreed scope, use this email structure.

**Subject:** Change order request — [Project name]

> Hi [Client name],
>
> Thanks for your message. I have reviewed your request for [describe the extra work, e.g. "three additional logo concepts and Instagram post templates"].
>
> This work falls outside the scope of work we agreed on [date], which covered [briefly restate the original deliverables].
>
> I am happy to add this as a change order. Here is a breakdown:
>
> **Additional work:**  
> — [Deliverable 1, e.g. "Three additional logo concepts"]  
> — [Deliverable 2, e.g. "Instagram post template pack (10 templates)"]  
>
> **Additional cost:** £[amount]  
> **Estimated turnaround:** [X days]  
>
> This would be invoiced separately, with payment due before I begin the additional work (or Net 14, depending on your preference).
>
> Let me know if you would like me to proceed, and I will send a formal change-order document for your approval.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

This is polite, professional, and assumes the client will pay. Most clients do.

## Before you send your scope of work — checklist

✓ Every deliverable has a clear definition of what "done" looks like  
✓ Common extras are listed in the "Out of scope" section  
✓ Revision rounds are defined (and limited to 2–3 rounds)  
✓ Timeline includes client feedback deadlines  
✓ Change-order process is explained  
✓ Payment schedule matches your cashflow needs  
✓ Acceptance criteria are clear  
✓ Both you and the client will sign or confirm approval in writing  

## When clients refuse to sign a scope of work

Most clients will sign a scope of work without pushback. It protects them as much as it protects you.

But occasionally, a client will say: "We do not sign these. Just send an email summary."

Here is what to do.

### Option 1: Send the scope of work as an email and ask for written confirmation

Paste the scope of work into an email and finish with:

> "Please reply to confirm you agree with the scope, deliverables, and timeline above. Once I receive your confirmation, I will begin work."

When they reply "Yes, confirmed," you have a written agreement. That is legally binding in the UK.

### Option 2: Attach the scope of work to your proposal

If you send a proposal before the contract, include the scope of work as an appendix. When the client accepts the proposal, they accept the scope.

For a full guide to writing proposals that convert, see [how to write a freelance proposal](/blog/how-to-write-a-freelance-proposal).

### Option 3: Walk away

If a client refuses to confirm scope in any form — no signature, no email, no proposal acceptance — that is a red flag.

Clients who will not agree to clear deliverables upfront are the same clients who demand endless revisions and unpaid extras later.

You can choose to work with them anyway. But you do so at your own risk.
