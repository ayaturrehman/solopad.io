Your client wants work to start tomorrow. The brief is clear, the timeline is tight, and you are ready to go. But no money has changed hands yet.

You need a deposit. Not next week. Not when the first milestone is done. Now — before you open Figma, before you write the first line of code, before you commit a single hour to this project.

A deposit protects your cashflow, filters out clients who are not serious, and makes sure you are not working for free if the project falls apart halfway through.

This guide gives you three copy-paste UK deposit invoice templates with GBP amounts, explains what fields to include, shows you how to reflect the deposit on your final balance invoice, and includes email scripts for when clients push back or ask for their deposit back.

## What a deposit invoice is (and why UK freelancers need one)

A **deposit invoice** (also called an upfront invoice or advance payment invoice) requests partial payment before work begins. It is not the final invoice — it is the first invoice in a two-part or multi-part payment structure.

**Example structure:**

✓ **Deposit invoice (40%):** £1,200 due before work starts  
✓ **Final invoice (60%):** £1,800 due upon project completion  

The deposit reduces your risk. If the client disappears mid-project, you have at least been paid for some of the time you invested. If the client cancels before you start, you can return the deposit (if it was refundable) or keep it (if it was non-refundable and stated clearly upfront).

### Deposit invoice vs proforma invoice

A **proforma invoice** is a quote that looks like an invoice but is not a request for payment. It shows what the client will owe if they proceed. It is used for customs, budgeting, or internal approvals.

A **deposit invoice** is a real invoice requesting real payment. It has a due date, a payment amount, and it must be paid before you start work.

Do not confuse the two. If you want money upfront, send a deposit invoice, not a proforma.

### Deposit invoice vs final invoice

The **deposit invoice** covers the upfront percentage. The **final invoice** (or **balance invoice**) covers the remainder.

The final invoice should reference the deposit invoice and deduct the deposit amount so the client sees exactly what they still owe.

## When to require a deposit — the decision tree

Not every project needs a deposit. But most do.

**Require a deposit when:**

✓ The project is over **£1,000**  
✓ The client is **new** and you have no payment history with them  
✓ The project requires **materials or subcontractors** you need to pay upfront  
✓ The timeline is **longer than two weeks** and you will invest significant time before the first milestone  
✓ The client has **slow payment processes** (large corporates, public sector) and you need cashflow now  

**You can skip a deposit when:**

✓ The client is a **repeat customer** who always pays on time  
✓ The project is **under £500** and you can afford the risk  
✓ You are invoicing **weekly or fortnightly** on a retainer and cashflow is already predictable  

If you are unsure, ask for a deposit. Serious clients expect it. Bad clients resist it. That resistance is useful information.

## How much deposit to charge — UK norms by discipline

There is no legal rule. The norm is **30–50%** depending on the industry and project risk.

### Designers (brand, web, graphic)

**40–50%** is standard.

Design projects are front-loaded. You do discovery, concepts, and revisions before the client sees the final result. A 50% deposit protects that upfront investment.

### Developers and technical work

**40–50%** is common, or milestone-based invoicing.

For a £6,000 build, you might invoice £2,400 upfront (40%), £1,800 at mid-point (30%), and £1,800 on delivery (30%).

### Consultants and strategists

**30–40%** is typical.

Consulting work is often delivered in phases. A 30% deposit covers the discovery phase and signals commitment.

### When to charge more than 50%

✓ The client is high-risk (new, no references, vague brief)  
✓ You need to pay for materials, licences, or subcontractors upfront  
✓ The project is very large and you cannot afford to carry the cashflow risk  

**Example:** A £12,000 website build with a new overseas client might justify a 60% deposit (£7,200) to protect you if payment becomes difficult.

### When to accept less than 30%

Only when the client is trusted and low-risk. If a repeat client asks for 20% upfront because of their internal approval process, you can accept that if your cashflow allows it.

## What to include on a UK deposit invoice — field checklist

A deposit invoice follows the same GOV.UK requirements as any other invoice, but the description must make it clear this is a deposit, not the full amount.

### Required fields

✓ **Unique invoice number** (e.g. INV-2026-045)  
✓ **Invoice date**  
✓ **Due date** (usually "due on receipt" or within 7 days)  
✓ **Your business name and address**  
✓ **Your contact details** (email or phone)  
✓ **Client name and address**  
✓ **Client billing contact or email**  
✓ **Clear description** stating this is a deposit and what percentage of the total  
✓ **Deposit amount in GBP**  
✓ **Total project fee** (so the client knows what the full cost is)  
✓ **Remaining balance and when it will be invoiced**  
✓ **Payment method** (sort code and account number)  
✓ **VAT details** (if you are VAT registered)  

### Helpful optional fields

✓ Project reference or name  
✓ Purchase order number (if the client provided one)  
✓ Note confirming work will not start until deposit is received  
✓ Whether the deposit is refundable or non-refundable (if stated in your contract)  

**Source:** [GOV.UK — Invoicing and taking payment from customers](https://www.gov.uk/invoicing-and-taking-payment-from-customers)

## How to show the deposit on the final balance invoice

When you send the final invoice, you need to show:

1. The total project value  
2. The deposit already paid  
3. The balance still owed  

Use a **negative line item** to deduct the deposit.

## Refundable vs non-refundable deposits — what to say on the invoice

Most UK freelancers use **non-refundable deposits** once work begins. This is standard practice and protects you if the client cancels mid-project.

### Non-refundable deposit wording

Include this note on your deposit invoice or in your contract:

> This deposit is non-refundable once work begins. If you cancel the project after work has commenced, the deposit will not be returned. If you cancel before work begins, the deposit will be refunded in full.

### Refundable deposit wording

If you want to offer a refundable deposit (less common), be specific about the conditions:

> This deposit is refundable if: (1) you cancel the project before work begins, or (2) we are unable to deliver the agreed work due to circumstances on our side. The deposit is not refundable if you cancel after work has commenced.

### Where to state this

✓ In your contract (before the client signs)  
✓ On the deposit invoice itself (as a note or line item)  
✓ In the email you send with the deposit invoice  

Do not leave it ambiguous. If you do not state whether the deposit is refundable, UK contract law may treat it as refundable by default, which is not what you want.

**Disclaimer:** This is a simplified summary. For complex deposit terms or large sums, consult a solicitor. This guide is not legal advice.

## Email scripts — how to send a deposit invoice and handle pushback

### Script 1 — Sending the deposit invoice (new client)

**Subject:** Deposit invoice — [Project name]

> Hi [Client name],
>
> Thank you for confirming the project. Attached is the deposit invoice (£[amount]) to secure your project start date.
>
> Once the deposit is received, I will schedule our kickoff meeting and begin work on [deliverable]. The remaining balance of £[amount] will be invoiced upon project completion and is due within 14 days (Net 14).
>
> You can pay by bank transfer using the details on the invoice. Let me know if you need any other payment method, and I will arrange it.
>
> Looking forward to working with you.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

### Script 2 — When a client asks why a deposit is needed

**Subject:** Re: Deposit invoice — [Project name]

> Hi [Client name],
>
> Thanks for your question. Deposits are standard practice in freelance projects. They protect both sides by confirming commitment before work begins.
>
> For me, the deposit ensures I can dedicate time to your project without cashflow risk if circumstances change. For you, it locks in my availability and guarantees I will prioritise your work.
>
> The deposit is [X]% of the total project fee and is non-refundable once work begins. If you need to cancel before I start, I will refund it in full.
>
> Let me know if you have any other questions, and I will be happy to explain further.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

### Script 3 — When a client refuses to pay a deposit

**Subject:** Re: Deposit invoice — [Project name]

> Hi [Client name],
>
> I understand deposits can feel like an extra step, but they are a standard part of how I work — and how most freelancers work in the UK.
>
> Without a deposit, I cannot dedicate time to your project ahead of other paid work. I hope you understand.
>
> If a deposit is not possible on your side, we can discuss milestone invoicing instead (e.g. smaller invoices at each project stage). Let me know if that works better for you.
>
> Otherwise, I am happy to revisit this project when your budget process allows for deposits.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

If the client still refuses, you have three options:

1. **Accept the risk** and start work without a deposit (not recommended unless the client is trusted)  
2. **Offer milestone invoicing** (invoice smaller amounts more frequently)  
3. **Walk away** (if the client will not pay a deposit, that is a red flag)

Serious clients pay deposits without fuss. The ones who resist are often the ones who will pay late or not at all.

### Script 4 — When a client asks for their deposit back mid-project

**Subject:** Re: Deposit refund request

> Hi [Client name],
>
> Thanks for your message. I understand circumstances have changed on your side.
>
> As stated in our agreement and on the deposit invoice, the deposit is non-refundable once work has commenced. I began work on [date] and have already invested [X hours / completed Y deliverables].
>
> If you would like to pause the project rather than cancel, I can hold the deposit and we can resume when you are ready. Let me know what works best for you.
>
> Best,  
> [Your name]

If the client insists they want a refund and threatens to escalate, consult a solicitor. Do not refund a non-refundable deposit unless you are legally obligated to.

## Checklist before you start work without a paid deposit

If you are about to start work and the deposit has not cleared, ask yourself:

✓ Has the client confirmed they have sent payment?  
✓ Do you have proof in writing (email or message) that they sent it?  
✓ Have you checked your bank account to confirm the deposit has arrived?  
✓ If you start work now and they cancel tomorrow, can you afford to lose the unpaid hours?  
✓ Is this client trusted, or are you taking a risk you cannot afford?  

**Rule:** Do not start work until the deposit has cleared in your bank account. "I sent it this morning" is not the same as "It has arrived."

Bank transfers in the UK are usually instant (Faster Payments), but they can take 1–2 hours during busy periods. Wait.

## When to use milestone invoicing instead of a single deposit

For projects longer than four weeks or over £5,000, consider **milestone invoicing** instead of a single deposit and final invoice.

**Example milestone structure (£6,000 website build):**

✓ **Milestone 1 (start):** 40% — £2,400 (deposit)  
✓ **Milestone 2 (mid-point):** 30% — £1,800 (after design approval)  
✓ **Milestone 3 (delivery):** 30% — £1,800 (after final handoff)  

Smaller invoices are easier for clients to approve and keep your cashflow healthy throughout the project.

## VAT on deposit invoices — the rules

If you are VAT registered, you must charge VAT on the deposit at the same rate as the final invoice (usually 20% standard rate).

**Example:**

Total project fee: £3,000 (net)  
Deposit (40%): £1,200 (net)  
VAT on deposit (20%): £240  
**Deposit invoice total:** £1,440 (gross)  

Final invoice (60%): £1,800 (net)  
VAT on final (20%): £360  
**Final invoice total:** £2,160 (gross)  

**Total project cost including VAT:** £3,600

### If you are not VAT registered

Do not charge VAT. Show "Not applicable" or "Not VAT registered" on the invoice if the client asks.

**VAT registration threshold:** You must register for VAT if your taxable turnover exceeds **£90,000** in a rolling 12-month period (as of 1 September 2026).

**Source:** [GOV.UK — VAT registration](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat)

## Common mistakes that delay deposit payments

**Vague invoice description.** Writing "Deposit — £1,200" tells the client nothing. Write "Deposit — 40% of £3,000 total project fee" so they understand what the full cost is and what remains.

**No due date.** Always state when the deposit is due. "Due on receipt" is clearest for deposits. If the client needs 7 days to process payment, write "Due 14 September 2026" and adjust your project start date accordingly.

**Not stating when work will start.** Add a note: "Work will commence once this deposit is received." This sets the boundary. No deposit, no work.

**Charging VAT when you are not registered.** Only charge VAT if you are VAT registered. If you are not, leave the VAT line blank or write "Not applicable."

**Starting work before the deposit clears.** The client says "I sent it this morning" and you start work. Two days later, the payment has not arrived. Now you are chasing and you have already invested hours. Wait for the money to clear before you open your design file or code editor.

**No paper trail.** Save a copy of every deposit invoice you send. Log when you sent it, when it was due, and when it was paid. If a dispute arises later, you need proof.
