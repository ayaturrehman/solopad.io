## What are terms and conditions (and how do they differ from a contract)?

**Terms and conditions** (also called **terms of business** or **standard terms**) are the general rules that apply to all work you do for clients. They cover payment terms, revisions, liability, cancellation, and IP. They apply to every job unless you replace them with a specific project contract.

**A contract** (or **statement of work**) is a project-specific agreement that defines the exact deliverables, milestones, fees, and timeline for one job. It replaces or sits alongside your standard terms.

**Website terms and conditions** are the legal pages on your website (privacy policy, cookie policy, acceptable use) that govern how visitors use your site. They do NOT cover the services you provide to clients.

### The difference at a glance

| Type | What It Covers | When You Use It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Website terms and conditions** | How visitors use your website (browsing, cookies, links, user content) | Always published on your website for site visitors |
| **Standard terms of business** | Payment, revisions, liability, cancellation, IP for all client work | Every quote or proposal you send |
| **Project contract / statement of work** | Specific deliverables, timeline, and fee for one project | High-value or complex projects where scope needs to be detailed |

**Key point:** Standard terms of business are your default. Use them for every job. Add a project contract or statement of work when the project is large, complex, or needs detailed scope definition.

## When you need terms and conditions vs a contract — the decision test

Not every freelance project needs a detailed contract. Here is when to use standard terms, when to use a contract, and when to use both.

### Use STANDARD TERMS OF BUSINESS when:

✓ You quote repeat clients or new clients on similar work (e.g. logo designs, website builds, article commissions)  
✓ The project is straightforward and low-value (under £2,000)  
✓ You send quotes or proposals rather than detailed contracts  
✓ You want one set of terms that applies to all jobs without rewriting them each time  

**Example:** You are a graphic designer. You quote £800 for a brand refresh. You send the quote with a link to your standard terms. The client accepts, pays the deposit, and you start work. The standard terms cover payment, revisions, and IP transfer.

### Use a PROJECT CONTRACT when:

✓ The project is over £2,000  
✓ The scope is complex or has multiple phases  
✓ The client wants detailed deliverables, milestones, and payment schedule  
✓ The project involves intellectual property you want to protect (e.g. software, original research, trade secrets)  
✓ The client is a corporate or public sector body that requires a formal contract  

**Example:** You are building a web application for £15,000 over 12 weeks. You sign a contract that defines the deliverables (user authentication, admin dashboard, API integration), milestones, payment schedule (30% deposit, 40% at functional prototype, 30% at launch), and IP transfer terms.

### Use BOTH (standard terms + project contract) when:

✓ You want standard protections (payment terms, liability cap, confidentiality) to apply to every job, PLUS a specific contract that defines the scope for this project  
✓ You work with repeat clients on different projects  
✓ You want to avoid rewriting the same clauses in every contract  

**Example:** Your standard terms cover payment (Net 14), late payment interest, liability cap, confidentiality, and IP transfer on full payment. Your project contract defines the specific deliverables, fee, and timeline for this job. The contract references your standard terms: "This statement of work is subject to the Supplier's standard terms of business, available at [URL]."

**Decision test:**

- **Simple, repeat, low-value work?** → Standard terms only  
- **Complex, high-value, detailed scope?** → Project contract (which may incorporate your standard terms by reference)  
- **Want both flexibility and protection?** → Standard terms that apply to all jobs + project-specific scope documents  

## What must be in your UK terms and conditions — clause checklist

Your standard terms of business should be clear, enforceable, and cover the situations where disputes arise. Here is what to include.

### Required clauses

✓ **Definitions and parties** — Who "you" (the freelancer or limited company) and "the Client" are  
✓ **How the contract forms** — When the terms become binding (e.g. when the client accepts your quote in writing or pays the deposit)  
✓ **Which document wins if they conflict** — E.g. if the quote says one thing and the terms say another, which takes priority (usually: quote > terms > email)  
✓ **Services and scope** — General description of what you do (you define specifics in each quote or statement of work)  
✓ **Change requests** — How scope changes are priced and approved  
✓ **Client responsibilities** — What the client must provide (content, feedback, approvals, access) and by when  
✓ **Fees, deposits, and expenses** — How you charge (per project, hourly, retainer), whether you require deposits, who pays expenses  
✓ **VAT** — Whether your prices include or exclude VAT, and what happens if you register for VAT mid-project  
✓ **Payment terms** — When payment is due (e.g. Net 14, Net 30)  
✓ **Late payment rights** — B2B statutory interest and fixed compensation under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998  
✓ **Revisions** — How many rounds are included and what counts as a revision vs a scope change  
✓ **Cancellation and kill fee** — What you are owed if the client cancels before completion  
✓ **Intellectual property** — When IP transfers (usually on full payment), whether you retain portfolio rights  
✓ **Confidentiality** — Both sides keep sensitive information private  
✓ **Liability cap and exclusions** — Limit your liability to the fee paid (or a multiple), except for fraud, death, or personal injury  
✓ **Termination** — Notice period and what happens to work in progress  
✓ **Data protection** — UK GDPR compliance (one short clause)  
✓ **Force majeure** — What happens if you cannot work due to illness, emergency, or events beyond your control  
✓ **Notices** — How to send formal notices (email, address)  
✓ **Governing law** — England and Wales (or Scotland, or Northern Ireland)  
✓ **Entire agreement** — These terms plus the quote or contract are the whole agreement, superseding prior discussions  

**Optional but useful:**

✓ Right to work for others during the project (no exclusivity)  
✓ Suspension of work for non-payment  
✓ Right to use subcontractors (with confidentiality obligations)  
✓ Warranty disclaimers (if selling templates or software)  
✓ Dispute resolution (arbitration, mediation, or court)  

**Tip:** Keep your standard terms under 5 pages. Long legal documents scare small business owners. Clear, plain-English terms are more likely to be read and accepted.

## How to make your terms and conditions legally binding

Your terms and conditions are only enforceable if the client has agreed to them. Here is how to make them bind.

### 1. Share your terms BEFORE the client accepts your quote

The client must see your terms and agree to them **before** or **at the time** they accept your offer. You cannot impose terms after the client has already agreed to the work.

**How to share them:**

✓ **Link them in your quote or proposal email:** "This quote is subject to our standard terms of business, available at [URL]."  
✓ **Attach them as a PDF to the quote email**  
✓ **Include them in your online quote or proposal tool** with a tick-box: "I have read and agree to [Your Name]'s standard terms of business."  
✓ **Reference them on the quote document itself:** "All quotes are subject to our standard terms of business, which can be viewed at [URL]."  

### 2. Get written acceptance

The client must agree to your terms in writing. This can be:

✓ A signed quote or proposal that references your terms  
✓ An email reply saying "I accept your quote and terms"  
✓ A tick-box or online acceptance form  
✓ Payment of a deposit after you have shared the terms (payment implies acceptance)  

**Tip:** If the client accepts your quote verbally (phone or in-person meeting), send a confirmation email immediately: "Thanks for accepting our quote for [project]. As discussed, this project is subject to our standard terms of business, available at [URL]. Please confirm your acceptance in writing by replying to this email or paying the deposit invoice."

### 3. Watch for the 'battle of the forms' risk

If your client is a larger business, they may send you a **purchase order** with **their own terms and conditions** attached.

This creates a **battle of the forms** — whose terms win?

**Under English law:**

- The **last set of terms sent before work begins** usually governs  
- If you accept their purchase order without objection, their terms may override yours  

**What to do:**

✓ When you receive a purchase order with their terms, reply immediately: "Thank you for the purchase order. We note that you have attached your own terms and conditions. This project is subject to our standard terms of business, which we shared with our quote on [date]. Please confirm you accept our terms, or let's discuss any concerns."  
✓ Do not start work until the terms issue is resolved  
✓ If the client insists on their terms, read them carefully. Check for: exclusion of your late payment rights, overly broad liability clauses, IP transfer before payment, or indemnity clauses that make you liable for the client's mistakes  

**Source:** [Practical Law — Battle of the forms](https://uk.practicallaw.thomsonreuters.com/9-107-6457) (subscription required, but the principle is that the last terms sent before performance governs).

### 4. Reference your terms on each invoice

Once work has begun, reference your terms on the footer of each invoice:

**Invoice footer example:**

> Payment terms: Net 14 days. Late payment subject to statutory interest and recovery costs under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998. Full terms: [URL]

Or:

> Payment subject to our standard terms of business, available at [yourwebsite.com/terms]

**Important:** The invoice reference is a reminder. It does not create agreement to your terms if the client never agreed before work started. Always share and get acceptance before the project begins.

**See also:** [Quote template UK](/blog/quote-template-uk) for linking terms in quotes, and [Freelance invoice template examples](/blog/freelance-invoice-template-examples) for invoice wording.

## Consumer clients: Consumer Rights Act and cancellation rights

If you sell services to **individual consumers** (people buying for personal use, not for their business), additional protections apply under UK consumer law.

### Consumer Rights Act 2015: fairness and reasonable care

**What it means:**

1. **Unfair terms are not binding:** Contract terms that are **unfair** or **not transparent** may not be enforceable. Unfair terms include clauses that disproportionately favour the supplier, hidden fees, or terms that take away the consumer's legal rights.

2. **Services must be performed with reasonable care and skill:** You must carry out services to a standard that a reasonable person would expect from someone with your skills and experience.

3. **Services must be completed within a reasonable time:** If you do not agree a delivery date, you must complete the work within a reasonable time.

**What freelancers must do:**

✓ Make your terms **clear and easy to understand** (plain English, not dense legal jargon)  
✓ Do not hide important terms in small print  
✓ Do not include terms that unfairly remove the consumer's legal rights (e.g. "no refunds under any circumstances" is likely to be unfair)  
✓ State your fees clearly and upfront (no hidden charges)  
✓ Deliver work to a professional standard and within the agreed time (or a reasonable time if no deadline was agreed)  

**Source:** [Consumer Rights Act 2015](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/contents) and [Citizens Advice — Consumer Rights Act](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/if-you-need-more-help-about-a-consumer-issue/)

---

### The 14-day cancellation right (distance and off-premises contracts)

If you sell services to a **consumer** and the contract is made:

- **Online, by phone, or by email** (distance contract), or  
- **At the consumer's home, workplace, or anywhere that is not your business premises** (off-premises contract)  

Then the **Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013** give the consumer the right to cancel within **14 days** of the contract date, for any reason, without penalty.

**What this means for freelancers:**

✓ **The consumer can cancel for any reason within 14 days** and get a full refund of any payment made, unless you have already completed the work with the consumer's express agreement (see below)  
✓ **You must tell the consumer about the cancellation right** before the contract is formed (in your quote, proposal, or terms)  
✓ **If you want to start work within the 14-day cancellation period**, you must get the consumer's **express written request** to begin work immediately, and inform them that by making this request they lose the right to cancel if you complete the work during the cancellation period  

**Example wording for consumer terms:**

> **Cancellation right for consumers (individuals buying for personal use):**
>
> You have the right to cancel this contract within 14 days of the date you accept our quote, for any reason, and receive a full refund of any payment made.
>
> If you want us to start work within the 14-day cancellation period, you must make an express request in writing. By requesting that we start work immediately, you acknowledge that if we complete the work during the cancellation period, you will lose your right to cancel and will be liable for the full fee.
>
> To cancel, email us at [your email] stating that you wish to cancel.

**Source:** [Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/contents/made) and [GOV.UK — Online and distance selling for businesses](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses) and [Citizens Advice — Cancelling a service you've arranged](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/changed-your-mind/cancelling-a-service-youve-arranged/)

---

### B2B vs consumer: which rules apply?

| Client Type | Which Rules Apply | What You Must Do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Business client** (limited company, partnership, LLP, sole trader buying for business, public body) | Contract law, Late Payment of Commercial Debts Act 1998, UK GDPR | Your standard B2B terms apply. You can enforce late payment interest and fixed compensation. No 14-day cancellation right. |
| **Consumer** (individual buying for personal use) | Consumer Rights Act 2015, Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, UK GDPR | Terms must be fair and transparent. Consumer has 14-day cancellation right (unless they request you start work immediately and you complete it within 14 days). Services must be performed with reasonable care and skill. |

**How to tell:** If the client is buying for their business (even if they are a sole trader), they are a business client. If they are buying for personal use (e.g. family portrait, home office design), they are a consumer.

**This guide focuses on B2B terms.** If you sell to consumers, add a short consumer section to your terms explaining the 14-day cancellation right and how to cancel.

**Legal disclaimer:** This is general information, not legal advice. Consumer law is complex. For unusual or high-value consumer contracts, consult a solicitor or contact [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/) or the [Competition and Markets Authority (CMA)](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/competition-and-markets-authority).

## Worked example: brand designer, £3,600 quote, 40% deposit, two revision rounds

Here is how standard terms of business work in a real freelance project.

**Scenario:**

A self-employed brand designer trading as **Northline Studio** quotes £3,600 (exclusive of VAT; Northline is not VAT registered) to a B2B client, **Horizon Labs Ltd**, for a brand refresh (logo, colour palette, typography, brand guidelines document).

**Payment schedule:**

- 40% deposit: £1,440 (Invoice 1, due before work begins)  
- 60% final payment: £2,160 (Invoice 2, due on delivery of final files)  

**Payment terms:** Net 14 (within 14 days of invoice date). Late payment: statutory interest and £70 fixed compensation under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts Act 1998.

**Revisions:** Two rounds of design revisions included in the fee.

**How the terms protect Northline Studio:**

### Situation 1: Client goes silent for six weeks after first concept

Northline delivers the first logo concepts on 10 October. The client does not respond. Northline emails on 17 October (reminder). Still no response.

On 21 November (six weeks later), Northline refers to **Clause 5.3** of their standard terms:

> "If the Client delays providing feedback or materials for more than 30 days, we may treat the project as cancelled and invoice for work completed to date."

Northline sends a formal email:

> "It has been six weeks since we delivered the first concepts and we have not received feedback. Under our standard terms (Clause 5.3), we are treating this project as cancelled due to client delay. We will invoice for the work completed: initial research, brand audit, and first concept round (50% of the project). The cancellation invoice is £1,800 (total project £3,600 × 50%). The deposit of £1,440 has already been paid, so the balance due is £360. Payment is due within 14 days."

The client replies immediately, apologises, and provides feedback. Northline agrees to continue the project. Crisis avoided.

**What the terms did:** They gave Northline leverage to either cancel and get paid, or prompt the client to respond.

---

### Situation 2: Client asks for a third round of revisions

After two rounds of revisions (both included in the fee), the client asks for a third round: "Can we try a different colour palette and a script version of the logo?"

Northline refers to **Clause 9** of their standard terms:

> "Two rounds of design revisions included. Additional revisions: £300 per round."

Northline replies:

> "The two revision rounds included in the original quote are complete. A third round (new colour palette and script logo variant) is a scope change. Additional fee: £300. Please confirm in writing if you would like to proceed."

The client accepts. Northline invoices £300 separately once the third round is delivered.

**What the terms did:** They set a clear limit on included revisions and gave Northline the right to charge for additional work.

---

### Situation 3: Client pays 25 days late

Northline delivers the final files on 1 December and invoices £2,160 (final payment). Payment terms: Net 14 (due 15 December).

The client pays on 26 December (25 days after the invoice date, 11 days late).

Northline refers to **Clause 8** of their standard terms and invoices:

- **Statutory interest:** 8% + Bank of England base rate (assume base rate is 4.75%, so total is 12.75% per annum) on £2,160 for 11 days = £2,160 × 12.75% ÷ 365 × 11 = £8.31  
- **Fixed compensation:** £70 (invoice between £1,000 and £9,999.99)  
- **Total late payment charge:** £78.31  

Northline sends a polite invoice for £78.31 with a note: "Payment was received 11 days after the due date. Under our standard terms and the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998, we are entitled to statutory interest and fixed compensation as shown."

The client pays immediately and apologises. They set up Northline as a priority supplier to avoid future delays.

**What the terms did:** They gave Northline the legal right to charge for late payment and recover the cost of chasing payment.

---

**Final maths:**

- Original project fee: £3,600  
- Third revision round: £300  
- Late payment recovery: £78.31  
- **Total earned:** £3,978.31  

Without standard terms, Northline would have had no leverage to charge for the third round or recover late payment costs.

## Pre-send checklist: before you share your terms with clients

Use this checklist to make sure your terms are ready, complete, and legally sound.

✓ **Your details are correct** — Name (or company name), address, email, VAT number (if registered), company number (if limited company)  
✓ **Payment terms are clear** — Net 14 or Net 30, how to pay (bank transfer, PayPal, etc.)  
✓ **Late payment clause references the Late Payment Act** — Statutory interest rate formula (8% + BoE base rate), fixed compensation amounts (£40, £70, £100), right to suspend work  
✓ **Deposit percentage is stated** — E.g. 40% deposit required before work begins  
✓ **Revisions are defined and limited** — E.g. "Two rounds of revisions included. Additional rounds: £[X] each."  
✓ **Kill fee clause protects you** — Minimum payment if client cancels (e.g. 30% or 40% of total fee)  
✓ **IP transfers on full payment only** — Clause states clearly: "All IP rights transfer to the Client upon receipt of full payment."  
✓ **Liability is capped** — Limited to total fees paid (or a multiple), except for fraud, death, personal injury  
✓ **Confidentiality clause covers both sides** — You and the client both agree to keep sensitive information private  
✓ **Data protection clause complies with UK GDPR** — Short clause covering your obligations if you process personal data  
✓ **Governing law is stated** — England and Wales, Scotland, or Northern Ireland (depending on your location)  
✓ **Terms are in plain English** — Not dense legal jargon. A small business owner can read and understand them.  
✓ **Terms are published on your website** — At a stable URL (e.g. yoursite.com/terms) so you can link to them in quotes  
✓ **You have a process to get client agreement** — Tick-box, signed quote, or email confirmation before work starts  

**Tip:** If you are not sure whether your terms are legally sound, pay a solicitor for a one-hour contract review. It costs £150–£300 and can save you thousands if a dispute arises.

## FAQ

### Are terms and conditions legally binding in the UK?

Yes, if the client agrees to them before or at the time of placing an order. To make them legally binding:

✓ Share your terms before the client accepts your quote (link them in the proposal or quote email, or require acceptance at checkout)  
✓ Get written agreement or a signature  
✓ Reference them on each invoice  

If the client never saw or agreed to your terms, you cannot enforce them.

**Source:** English contract law principle: terms must be incorporated into the contract before or at the time of formation. See [Practical Law — Incorporation of terms](https://uk.practicallaw.thomsonreuters.com/Browse/Home/PracticalLaw/BrowseUK) (subscription required).

---

### Do I need terms and conditions and a contract?

It depends.

**Use standard terms of business** (one set that applies to every job) for repeat small clients or quoting situations where you send the same terms with every proposal.

**Use a project contract** for high-value, complex, or one-off projects where the scope, deliverables, and payment need to be specific.

**Use both:** Standard terms that apply to every job, plus a contract or statement of work for the specific project scope.

**See also:** [Freelance contract template guide](/blog/freelance-contract-template-guide) for full project contracts.

---

### Can I put terms and conditions on my invoice?

Yes, you can reference them on your invoice footer (e.g. "Full terms and conditions available at [URL]" or "Payment subject to terms at [link]").

However, to make them bind the client, you must share them and get agreement **before the work begins**, not after the invoice is sent.

The invoice reference reminds the client, but the terms need to have been accepted at the quote or contract stage.

---

### Do sole traders need terms and conditions?

Not legally required, but highly recommended.

Terms and conditions protect you from late payment, scope creep, liability claims, and disputes. Without terms, you rely on verbal agreements and the client's goodwill.

For business clients (B2B), terms protect your right to statutory interest and late payment compensation. For consumer clients, terms show you meet transparency and fairness standards.

---

### Can I use a website terms and conditions generator for my freelance services?

No. Website T&C generators create terms for website use, cookies, and user-generated content. They do NOT cover services you provide.

Freelance services need **standard terms of business** that cover: payment, scope, revisions, liability, IP, cancellation, and governing law.

Use a freelance-specific terms template (like the one in this guide), not a website T&C builder.

---

### What is the difference between terms and conditions and terms of business?

They are the same thing. "Terms and conditions" and "terms of business" both refer to the standard clauses that apply to your services.

Some freelancers call them "terms of business" to distinguish them from website terms and conditions. Use whichever label you prefer; the content is what matters.
